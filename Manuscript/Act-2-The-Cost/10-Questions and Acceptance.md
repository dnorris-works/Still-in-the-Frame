# Chapter 10: Questions and Acceptance

He asked on a Sunday.

Not formally — not with any of the weight the moment deserved or that she had expected, in all the years she had imagined it. He asked the way he asked most things: quietly, with room for her to answer or not answer, with no indication that there was a wrong response.

They were on his couch. She was reading. He had his sketchpad out — not drawing, just making the kind of loose marks he made when his hands needed to be doing something while his mind did something else. The city was grey outside. The heating was on.

"Can I ask you something?"

She looked up. "Yes."

"Tell me about the first time you knew. About yourself." He didn't look up from the sketchpad. "If you want to."

She set her book down.

She had thought about this conversation for years. Had drafted it in her head on the subway, in the dark before sleep, in the margins of her imagination where she let herself rehearse things she was never sure would happen.

"I was ten," she said. "I knew my body was different before I knew there was a word for it. I just knew. The way you know your own hands." She paused. "At sixteen I found the medical term. I was alone in the school library with the computer turned slightly away from the door."

He made a mark on the paper. Waited.

"It helped. Having a word. It also made it real in a way it hadn't been before." She pulled her knees up. "I told my mother."

"What did she say?"

"Nothing, really. She went very still, and then she said: 'You don't need to tell anyone else about that.' And then we never spoke about it again." She looked at the window. "She was trying to protect me. I think. I've decided to believe that."

"Okay," he said. Not arguing it. Just accepting her interpretation.

"My father knew. He must have — you don't raise a child without knowing. He just made the same choice she did. We all agreed, without ever saying so, not to see it." She paused. "That's a particular kind of loneliness. Being unseen by the people who are supposed to see you most."

He set the sketchpad down.

"And then there was someone," he said. Not a question — he already knew there had been someone. He was asking her to bring him into the room.

She had known this part was coming.

"Bret," she said. "He was a critic. Arts journalism — reviews, exhibition pieces, that kind of thing. We met at an opening. I knew his work before I knew him, which I think was part of why I trusted him." She paused. "I'd had sex before — a few times, with a few different people. Always the same way, always me in control of the angle, the light, keeping it brief. It would last a few weeks and then they'd want more — more light, different positions, slower — and I'd feel the exit coming and I'd take it. End things before anyone found anything." A small, humourless breath. "I got very good at that. Leaving before I got found."

He didn't say anything. Just listened.

"Bret was the same at first. A few weeks in, he started wanting more. I could feel myself about to do what I always did." She looked at her hands. "But I was twenty-five and I was so tired of it. Tired of leaving, tired of managing, tired of being alone with it. I thought: just once. Tell someone. So I told him. We'd only been together about a month."

Ethan was very still.

"I prayed first," she said. "I hadn't prayed in years and I prayed the night before I told him. I asked God to make it okay." She heard the flatness in her own voice, the old scar tissue around it. "He looked at me like I was something he'd stepped in. He said: 'What the fuck. You're a freak.' He said: 'You're not a real woman.' He said: 'You deceived me.'"

The room was quiet.

"He told me not to tell anyone else. That I would ruin myself." A pause. "And that if I did, he'd make sure everyone knew what I was — because he wasn't going to have people thinking he'd been with someone like me. He said it like a fact. Like he had the means to do it." She looked at her hands. "He did, too. He writes for the same publications that review the galleries I work with. He knows Dr. Bowen's name. He knows everyone I need to take me seriously." A beat. "And then I believed him for two years. Which is the part I'm still working on."

Ethan reached over and put his hand flat on the couch between them. Not grabbing her, not pulling her toward him. Just placing his hand where she could take it if she wanted to.

She took it.

"He was wrong," Ethan said. His voice was even, quiet, completely certain. "About every single thing he said. He was wrong about what you are and wrong about what you deserve and wrong about what it means to be a woman." A pause. "He was a coward who made his failure your problem. That's all he was."

She pressed her lips together.

"I know," she said. "I know that. It's getting louder than his voice, slowly."

"Good." He held her hand. "Tell me when it's not loud enough and I'll say it again."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, the kind that arrived when something landed in exactly the right place.

They sat with it for a while. The grey afternoon outside. His thumb moving slowly over the back of her hand.

After a moment she said: "There's one other thing."

He waited.

"I can't get pregnant. It's — part of the same picture. The biology of it." She said it the way she said most hard things: plainly, without softening it, looking at the window rather than at him. "I thought you should know."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Okay," he said.

She looked at him. "That's all?"

"Did you want me to say something else?"

She thought about it honestly. "No," she said. "That's the right answer."

He didn't make anything of it — didn't mourn it aloud or perform acceptance or ask her how she felt about it, which would have required her to answer a question she was still carrying privately. He just held her hand and let the fact exist in the room without making it into a scene.

She was still getting used to that. The way he received things.

"The God thing," she said eventually. "I lost that the same night. I had prayed and it hadn't worked and it was easier to decide he wasn't there than to decide he was there and didn't care."

"That makes sense."

"Does it?"

"Yes." He turned to look at her. "If you believed in someone who was supposed to love you, and they let that happen — walking away is the only dignified response."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Or," he said, carefully, "maybe it wasn't that God let it happen. Maybe God knew you needed something Bret couldn't give you."

He didn't say anything else. He let it sit there in the grey afternoon like something that didn't need to be decided today.

She looked at him.

Something moved through her that she hadn't expected — not warmth, not the quiet opening she might have predicted. Something harder than that. She felt it arrive in her chest before she understood what it was.

"That's a very tidy way to frame it," she said.

He looked up.

"God knew. God planned. Everything that happened was — what, necessary? Part of the design?" Her voice was even but something underneath it wasn't. "A man told me I was a freak. I lost two years to that. I stopped praying. I stopped believing anyone could—" She stopped. Stood up. "That's not a plan. That's not providence or whatever word you want to use for it. That was just a bad thing that happened because someone was cruel."

"Maya—"

"I'm not angry at you." She picked up her jacket from the chair. "I don't know what I am." She looked at him — his face open, not defensive, which somehow made it worse. "I just — I need to go. I need some air."

She left without explaining further because she didn't have an explanation. She was out the door and down the stairs and onto the street before she could name what had happened, and even then she couldn't quite reach it. Not anger at him. Not exactly. Something more like being offered a comfort she wasn't ready for and not having the grace to receive it quietly.

She walked six blocks without deciding to. The city was grey and ordinary around her.

She took out her phone twice. Put it away both times.

At some point she stopped walking and stood outside a newsagent's and looked at nothing in particular and understood that what she was actually angry about had nothing to do with what he'd said. It was older than that. Heavier than that. And he hadn't caused it, which meant walking out on him hadn't solved it, which meant she was going to have to go back.

She turned around.

---

She knocked. He opened the door without surprise, which was either very good or very annoying and she couldn't decide which.

"I don't know what that was," she said.

"I know."

"It wasn't about you."

"I know that too."

She stood in the doorway for a moment. "I'm not ready to believe God planned any of it. I might never be."

"Okay," he said. Just that.

She came back in. He made tea she didn't ask for. They sat at opposite ends of the couch and didn't touch and the grey afternoon continued outside and neither of them tried to fix what had happened or explain it away, and eventually she picked up her book again and he picked up his sketchpad and the room settled around them, imperfect and quiet and real.
