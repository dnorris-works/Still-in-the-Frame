# Chapter 15: Dr. Bowen

Dr. Bowen's office smelled of old paper and cold coffee, which was either a deliberate choice or the result of twenty years of the same habits. Maya had been in this office enough times to know that the coffee cup on the desk was rarely the same one from the beginning of the meeting, which meant Bowen drank more than she appeared to, which Maya found reassuring.

"Sit," Bowen said, without looking up from the pages in her hand.

Maya sat. She had printed the revised second chapter that morning — the section on concealment as protection and the section on concealment as cost, now in the order she'd decided on after the walk home on Wednesday. The print was in her bag. She waited.

Bowen read. She turned pages with the particular unhurried quality of someone for whom time moved differently — not slowly, just at her own pace, which happened to be deliberate. Maya had learned not to fill the silence.

Eventually Bowen set the pages down.

"You've sharpened the Voss argument considerably." She took off her glasses. "But I want to push on something. The central question of this thesis — what is it? Not the Voss question. Yours."

Maya had prepared for this. "Whether concealment in artistic practice is ultimately protective or destructive."

"Both, you're going to tell me."

"Both," Maya said. "It protects in the short term. It damages in the long term. The damage compounds."

Bowen tapped the pages. "That's the easy version. I want the harder one." She leaned back. "What are you actually arguing — that concealment is valid, or that it's costly? Because those are different essays."

Maya looked at her.

"It's both," Maya said. "But not equally."

"Go on."

"The concealment is valid at the point of origin. It's the only tool available. You can't ask someone to step out from behind the work before they know how to survive the exposure. The hiding is — it's not cowardice. It's the earliest form of honesty, the one you can sustain before you can sustain the full version." She paused. "But it has a ceiling. The artists I most admire — the ones the thesis is actually about — they all hit the ceiling. They all reached a point where the concealment cost more than it protected. And the ones who chose revelation — they were freer. Not immediately. Not without damage. But freer."

Bowen was quiet.

"The thesis isn't defending concealment," Maya said. "It's tracing the full arc. The hiding, the cost, and the choice."

Bowen picked up her glasses. Put them back on. Looked at Maya for a moment in the way she had — not unkindly, but with the focused attention of someone who was genuinely evaluating.

"That's the thesis," she said. "Write that sentence in the introduction. Exactly that. I want to hear it again."

Maya hesitated. She'd said it once and it had come out right, the way things sometimes did when you weren't trying, and she wasn't sure she could reproduce it.

"The hiding is valid at the start," she said. "And then — there's a ceiling. And the ones who chose—" She stopped. It wasn't coming out the same way. The precision was gone. "I can't—" She shook her head. "I said it better the first time."

"You did," Bowen said. "Write it down before you lose it."

They spent another forty minutes on the structure — the case study sequencing, the question of how much space Voss deserved relative to the two supporting artists, whether the conclusion was earning its final claim or merely restating the argument. Maya's pen moved across her notepad. The chapter was already reorganising itself in her head.

At the end, as she was gathering her things, Bowen said: "I had a conversation last week with a colleague in the theology faculty. Douglas Reid — he works on faith and aesthetics."

Maya looked up.

"He came across your Voss paper — the one from the postgraduate conference in March. He said he found it resonant in ways he hadn't expected." Bowen refilled her coffee cup from a thermos Maya hadn't noticed on the shelf. "He said theology does the same thing. Embeds truth in forms that protect it until the culture can receive it plainly. Hides it in plain sight, so to speak." A small pause. "I thought it might be worth a conversation, if you were interested. The faculty occasionally funds cross-disciplinary work."

"Thank you," Maya said. "I'll think about it."

She walked out of the building and down the steps and across the quad and didn't stop until she was on the far side of it, under the oak trees, where she stood for a moment with her bag over her shoulder and her breath making small clouds in the cold air.

*Theology does the same thing. Hides truth in plain sight.*

She had not thought about it in those terms. She had been thinking about art — about brushwork and formal strategy and the ethical weight of choosing concealment — and the argument had been, at some level, obviously about her own life, but she had kept it in the frame of the academic because that was how she could carry it.

She stood under the oak trees for a while.

The structure was the same. That was what she was turning over. Hiding something true in a form that protects it, waiting for the culture or the room or the person to be ready for the plain version — Voss had done it with paint. Her grandmother had done it with a green Bible and a quiet certainty that never argued because it didn't need to. The artist in the east corner had done it with collage and scripture and a figure standing in a doorway.

She thought about the iron door handle. The cold of it. The warmth she'd felt through the door.

But there was a note in her drawer in her own teenage handwriting and she had never thrown it away, and a theologian she had never met had read her paper and recognised something in it that she was only beginning to recognise herself.

He was in Gallery Four when she found him — the Hargreaves show opened in three days, and he had been in the museum every afternoon that week. He was up on the rolling ladder with a track light, his back to the room, the particular focused stillness she recognised from the darkroom and every other moment when his hands were doing something his mind had already finished with.

She stood in the archway and watched him work.

He finished the adjustment, checked the effect from the ladder, climbed down. Turned and saw her.

"Hey." No surprise. Just — glad.

"Hey."

He looked at her for a moment. "How'd it go with Bowen?"

"Good." She set her bag down on the bench along the wall. "She pushed on the thesis. Made me say what it's actually arguing."

"What is it actually arguing?"

Maya looked at the paintings on the wall — the Hargreaves pieces, large and abstract, the kind that organised themselves differently depending on where you were standing.

"That the hiding costs more than it protects," she said. "Eventually. Not at the start — at the start it's the only honest thing available. But there's a ceiling."

He was quiet.

"Bowen wanted the sentence written down before I lost it." She looked at him. "I don't think I'm going to lose it."

He crossed the gallery and stood beside her in front of the canvas. Not saying anything. Just there.

After a while she reached over and took his hand.

"The thesis is almost done," she said.

"I know."

He squeezed her hand once and didn't answer, because there was nothing to add to that, and he knew it.
