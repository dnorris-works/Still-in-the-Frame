# Chapter 16: The Threat

She saw him at seven forty-three.

She knew the time because she had checked her watch a few minutes earlier — an old habit at openings, tracking the arc of the evening, knowing when the room would peak and when it would begin to thin. The exhibition was called *Drawn Inward: New Acquisitions in Works on Paper*, and it was the Hidden Gallery's strongest show in two years, and Maya had been feeling good about it for most of the evening.

Then she looked up from a conversation with the gallery's assistant director and saw Bret Reynolds standing in the second room with a glass of wine and his press lanyard and the particular ease of someone who was exactly where he was supposed to be.

Her body registered him before her mind caught up — a cold drop through the chest, the reflexive assessment she'd spent three years trying to unlearn. He hadn't seen her yet. He was looking at the work: a large graphite piece, two figures, which he appeared to be evaluating with professional detachment.

He had every right to be here. He covered shows like this. She had known, in the abstract, that the world they both moved in was not large. She had not thought, until this moment, about what that meant in practice.

She kept her face where it was and finished the sentence she had been saying and made an excuse to the assistant director and crossed to the first room where Ethan was.

He was near the window with his camera, framing a shot of the crowd rather than the work. He lowered the camera when he saw her face.

"What's wrong?"

"Bret is in the second room," she said quietly. "He has a press lanyard. He's covering the show."

Ethan was still for a moment. "Okay."

"He hasn't seen me yet."

"Do you want to leave?"

She looked at him. "No." She straightened. "It's my show. I'm not leaving my show."

He nodded once. "Then we stay."

---

She managed the rest of the evening the way she managed things — with precision, moving through the rooms, speaking to the people she needed to speak to, keeping Bret in her peripheral awareness without looking at him directly. He moved through the rooms as a critic moves: pausing, assessing, making notes on his phone. He spoke to Celia briefly. He stood in front of three pieces for longer than the others.

He saw her at nine-fifteen. She felt the moment he did — the slight adjustment in his posture, the pause, the recalibration. She was in conversation with a collector from the north side and she didn't turn, and by the time she had finished and looked, he was in the other room again.

Ethan stayed close without hovering. He didn't ask questions. He just moved through the same rooms she moved through, at the same general distance, and when she needed to find him he was findable.

The last guests left at ten. The room was emptying — Celia, the assistant director, a few lingering collectors. Maya was standing near the entrance speaking to a woman who wanted to discuss the second acquisitions piece when she heard footsteps behind her and felt the particular quality of deliberate approach.

She wrapped up the conversation. Turned.

Bret was standing four feet away. Up close he looked older than she remembered — not dramatically, just the way three years shows up in a face. He had the lanyard still around his neck and his notebook in his hand and he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read from this angle, from this distance, from this much accumulated history.

"Good show," he said. "The Alderton piece in particular. Strong acquisition."

"Thank you."

"I've been following your work." A pause — the weight of it deliberate. "We should talk. About what I know."

The room had not changed around her but it felt smaller. Celia was near the back. The assistant director was stacking programmes. Ethan was — she could feel him crossing the room, arriving quietly beside her, saying nothing.

Bret glanced at Ethan once. Then back to Maya.

"You won't do that," Ethan said. His voice was even, low, completely certain. Not a question.

Bret looked at him with the faint assessment of someone deciding whether the obstacle was worth addressing. He had the measuring quality of a person accustomed to being the one with information, the one who knew more than the room.

"You don't know what I'll do," Bret said.

"I know what it would make you," Ethan said. "And so do you."

The silence stretched. Maya heard it filling with everything she had spent three years afraid of — the shape of his knowledge, the platform he had, the names he knew, the publications that would take the piece. She heard the echo of his kitchen and the specific weight of that wooden chair and the controlled voice that had been somehow worse than shouting.

She had stood in that kitchen alone. She was not alone in this room.

Then she heard herself speak.

"And if you do," she said, "I'll make sure everyone knows we were intimate." Her voice was level. No heat in it, no performance. Just the plain fact of it, laid down like a card she had been holding for three years without knowing it. "I have nothing to lose by saying it. You do."

The room was very quiet.

Bret looked at her. Something moved across his face — not quite surprise, more the expression of a man who had prepared for a version of this conversation and found the room arranged differently than he'd expected.

He said nothing for a long moment. Then he put his notebook in his jacket pocket and looked at the piece on the nearest wall — the graphite figures, two of them, facing away from each other — and looked back at Maya.

"Good show," he said again.

He walked to the door. It swung shut behind him.

---

She didn't say anything in the gallery. Celia came to say goodnight and she said the right things and shook the right hands and watched the last of the room empty out. Ethan packed his camera. The assistant director turned up the work lights and the magic of the evening went back to being just fixtures on a track.

In the car she sat with her hands in her lap and the city moving past and said nothing for four blocks.

"He's going to think about it," she said.

"Yes."

"He still has the platform. He still knows who to call."

"Yes."

"What I said — " She stopped. "I don't know if that was the right thing."

Ethan drove. He didn't answer immediately, which was how she knew he was actually thinking about it.

"You said the true thing," he said finally. "That's not always the same as the safe thing." A pause. "But it was right. He needed to know you're not defenceless."

She looked out the window. The street lights moved across the glass.

She had stood in his kitchen three years ago and said *okay* and put her jacket on one arm at a time and walked out the door and down three flights of stairs and kept walking for forty-five minutes before the shaking started. Tonight she had stood in a room full of people and said the true thing in a level voice and not shaken at all.

That was different. She sat with how different it was.

"Thank you for being there," she said.

"I was always going to be there."

She looked at him. He was watching the road, his hands easy on the wheel, the same way he stood in front of work he was genuinely looking at — unhurried, present, not going anywhere.

She faced forward. The city kept moving past.

The fear was still there. It would be there tomorrow and possibly for some time after. But it shared the room now with something else — the knowledge that she had spoken, had not been destroyed by speaking, had looked at the thing that had been holding her hostage for three years and told it exactly what she had.

She sat with both things. The city thinned. The lights went on moving.
