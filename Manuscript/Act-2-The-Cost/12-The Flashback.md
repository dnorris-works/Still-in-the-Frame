# Chapter 12: The Flashback

She got home at eleven-fifteen.

Ethan had offered to come up. She had said no — not coldly, not as a withdrawal, just as a true thing: she needed to be alone with what the night had stirred in her, and he had understood that without requiring an explanation, which was one of the things about him that still surprised her.

She didn't turn the main lights on. Just the lamp by the bookcase, the low warm one that made the apartment look like evening even when it was night. She sat on the couch in her gallery clothes — the dark green dress, the good earrings, her coat still half-on — and didn't move.

The speech was still in her head. *There's something it costs you, to keep hiding the true thing. It compounds.*

She had meant Elara Voss when she wrote that. She had meant Andile Dlamini and his early figure work and the decade he spent painting landscapes before he came back to the body. She had meant every artist in that exhibition, every person who had taken the long way around to their own truth.

And now, in the quiet of her apartment, with the lamp throwing its small circle of light and the city doing its late-night thing outside, Bret's voice arrived.

---

*You're a freak.*

She had been sitting in his kitchen. She could still feel the specific weight of that chair — wooden, hard against the backs of her thighs, the kind of chair that was fine for twenty minutes and uncomfortable after. The kitchen had smelled of coffee and something he'd cooked for dinner, something with garlic. The window above the sink looked onto a brick wall. She had looked at that wall a lot while she was talking.

She had planned what she was going to say for a week. She had rehearsed it on the subway, in the shower, lying awake at two in the morning with her heart already going. She had wanted it to be right. She had wanted to hand him something true and have him hold it carefully.

She had prayed first. She hadn't prayed in years but she had prayed that morning, in the bathroom with the tap running so her flatmate couldn't hear. She had asked God, if he was there, to make it okay. To make Bret be the person she believed he was. To let this be the time it worked.

Bret had been quiet for a moment when she finished. He had looked at the table. She had watched him and waited, her hands very still in her lap, her whole body waiting.

*You're not a real woman.*

She remembered the specific sequence of his face. The way understanding had moved across it — slowly, like weather — and then the second thing that replaced it. She had seen disgust before, in other contexts. She had never had it directed at her, not like that. Not from someone who had held her hand. His voice when it came was controlled, which was somehow worse than if he had shouted. Controlled meant considered. Controlled meant this was what he had decided after thinking about it.

*You deceived me. I'm not going to have people knowing I was with someone like you.*

She had sat very still. She had not cried in his kitchen. She had said: okay. She had stood up and picked up her jacket from the back of the chair and put it on slowly, one arm at a time, because her hands needed something to do. She had walked to the door, and through it, and down the three flights of stairs and onto the street, and she had kept walking for forty-five minutes without knowing where she was going, and it was only when she stopped — outside a newsagent's, staring at nothing — that the shaking started.

She had not prayed again after that. There had seemed no point. If God was there, he had heard her and sent her Bret anyway. If God was there and that was his answer, she wanted nothing to do with him. It was cleaner to decide he wasn't there at all.

She had tried the other answer once. She had been nineteen — two years after the school library, one year into university, still carrying the word the doctors had used and the smaller words she had found herself, none of which quite fit. A campus chaplain. She had not gone looking for a verdict; she had gone because she was tired and because the door had been open and because she had been told, somewhere, that these people were safe.

He had been kind. That was the thing she could never fully set aside — he had been genuinely kind, the way a person is kind when they believe they are helping. He had listened without interrupting. He had made tea. His office had a view of a courtyard, and she had looked at it while she talked, and when she finished he had sat for a moment and then opened his Bible and read from it — not unkindly, not with judgment in his voice, only with the careful tone of someone delivering a difficult truth as gently as they could.

The word he had used was *disordered*. Not as cruelty. As category. As a medical term delivered in a pastoral voice, the way you might tell someone their results had come back and the news was not good but there were options. He had said that her condition — he had used the word condition — placed her outside the ordinary design, and that this was a cross to carry rather than a sin to confess, and that the church would walk with her in it. He had said she was called, given her circumstances, to a life of celibacy — not as punishment, he had been clear about this, but as gift. As the vocation that her body, in his framing, made available to her.

She had looked at the courtyard. A pigeon on the stone wall. Two students crossing below with their heads down.

She had said: thank you. She had meant: I understand. What she had understood was this — that the kindest version of the answer was still the same answer. That whether the voice was cruel or careful, the conclusion was identical: there was no version of her that the institution had room for. She was a category error. She could be accompanied, she could be prayed for, she could be walked alongside — but she could not be married, she could not be loved in the ordinary sacramental sense, she could not be what she was and also fully belong.

She had walked out of his office and down the corridor and out into the courtyard and stood in it for a long time.

So when she prayed, three years later, in the bathroom with the tap running — she had not been naive. She had known what the institution said. She had been asking God to be larger than the institution. She had been asking, very specifically, for Bret to be the proof that the chaplain had been wrong.

Bret had not been the proof.

That was the night she lost two things. She hadn't understood that until recently — had felt it only as one large undifferentiated wound. Sitting in her apartment now, in her good earrings, with the gallery speech still warm in her chest, she could finally separate them.

She sat with that for a while.

---

She called Ethan at quarter past midnight. She hadn't planned to. She had been sitting in the near-dark thinking about Bret's kitchen and the specific quality of that silence and then she had her phone in her hand and his name on the screen and she pressed call before she could talk herself out of it.

He answered on the second ring.

"Hey." No sleep in his voice. He had been awake.

"I'm okay," she said, which was both true and not. "I just — the night brought something up. I didn't want to sit with it alone."

"Tell me."

So she told him. Not the whole story — he already had the whole story, from the Sunday afternoon on his couch when she had handed it all to him. But the feeling of it tonight, the way the gallery speech had opened something and then the memory had walked through the door. The specific sound of Bret's voice in her head. The two losses on the same night that she had only just understood were two.

Ethan listened. He didn't say anything until she was finished.

"You gave thirty people something real tonight," he said. "In that room. That's yours. He can't touch it."

She pressed her lips together.

"I know."

"And the prayer thing." A pause. "You asked God to make it okay, and it went wrong. I get why you walked away from that." Another pause, shorter. "But Maya. Look at where you are now."

She looked around her apartment. The lamp. The good earrings. Her coat still half-on.

"I'm sitting in the dark in my good dress," she said.

"You're sitting in the dark in your good dress having just given a talk that moved people, after a year that would have finished most people off." His voice was even. "You're still here. You're more than still here."

She said nothing for a moment.

"He said I wasn't a real woman," she said.

"I know."

"I keep waiting for that to stop mattering."

"It might not stop mattering," he said. "It might just get quieter. The way it has been." A pause. "You said it was getting louder than his voice, slowly."

"It is." She exhaled. "Tonight it just — lost some ground."

"Yeah." She could hear him shift, settle. "You want me to come over?"

She thought about it. The lamp, the quiet, the thing she needed to do with this evening before she slept — hold it, let it settle, let the gallery and the memory and the two losses on the same night sit together without forcing them into an answer.

"No," she said. "I just needed to hear your voice."

"Okay." A beat. "I'm here."

She sat with that for a little while. He stayed on the line without speaking, which was what she needed — not more words, just the fact of him there. The lamp threw its small circle. The city did its quiet late-night business outside. She could hear him breathing, and occasionally the small sounds of him settling, and it was enough. It was more than enough.

At some point she got up and took her coat off and hung it properly on the hook. She made a cup of tea she didn't particularly want. She carried it back to the couch and sat down and tucked her feet under her.

"Still there?" she said.

"Still here."

She drank the tea. He waited.

"Thank you," she said eventually.

"Go to sleep, Maya."

She almost smiled. "Night."

She put the phone down and sat in the quiet for a while longer. The memory of Bret's kitchen still present, but smaller now, the way a thing becomes smaller when you say it aloud to someone who doesn't flinch.

She thought: *the same night. Two things gone.*

She didn't pray. But she didn't not, either.

She went to bed at one.
