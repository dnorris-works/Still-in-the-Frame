# Chapter 18: The Day After the Yes

He woke early on the Wednesday and lay still for a moment before he understood what was different.

Nothing was different. That was the thing. The room was the same room. The flat files were in the studio, where they had always been. The world had not rearranged itself to mark what he'd said at two in the morning.

He made coffee and stood at the kitchen window. The city was doing what it always did — grey and continuous, the early commuters below in their coats. He watched them and thought about Celia's spring slot, about the specificity of *asking Celia*, about the moment a conversation held in the dark became a calendar date that other people knew about.

He was not afraid of it. He needed to be clear with himself about that.

He was simply noticing that the morning was going to be what it always was, and that he was going to have to live in it carrying the certainty he'd declared, and that those were different things.

---

He went to the museum at nine to finish the east wall calibration. He moved through the work methodically — the familiar satisfaction of a physical problem solved in a quiet room.

At half eleven Marcus Pryor came through the staff entrance.

Marcus worked acquisitions. They had the collegial but not close relationship of two people at the same institution for several years without ever finding much overlap. Affable. The sort of person who always knew the most recent institutional news.

"Ethan." He stopped with his coffee. "Good news about the spring show. Celia mentioned it this morning — the Calloway-Vasquez thing." He glanced over. "That's what she called it. She seems pleased."

Ethan kept his voice even. "When did she mention it?"

"Corridor conversation. Ten minutes ago." Marcus shrugged pleasantly, looking at the Hartley canvases without strong opinions about them. "Vasquez is one of ours, isn't she? Tours and education?"

"Part-time."

"So this is portraits of her? Your photographs?" The uncomplicated curiosity of someone who didn't realise he was standing in the middle of anything. "What's the story?"

The question sat in the air.

He had rehearsed this. Not in so many words, but he had sat in the hard version — the show open, strangers asking, someone at a private view saying *what is she?* with the inflection of a person who needed the information before they knew how to look. He had sat in that version and not flinched.

This was not that version. This was before the show, before Maya had chosen her frame, before a single word had been agreed. And Celia had told Marcus something in a corridor, and Marcus was standing here with his coffee.

"It's a project about visibility," he heard himself say.

Even as he said it he knew it was insufficient. Not wrong. But a closed door that invited the next question.

"Right." Marcus nodded. "Like — social visibility? Identity?"

"Something like that." A pause. "It's Maya's story to tell."

"Sure. Though Celia made it sound like there was a specific subject matter—"

"Still her story." He said it more cleanly this time. "You'll know everything when the show opens."

Marcus raised both hands, good-natured. "Fair enough. Nice to have something to look forward to in March." He nodded at the Hartley wall and left.

---

Ethan stayed in the gallery after he'd gone.

He stood in the morning light — the light he had fixed, technically correct now, doing exactly what he'd needed it to do — and understood that he had said the right thing and done it poorly.

*It's Maya's story to tell.* True. The correct answer. Said too late, after one answer that was insufficient, because he had not been ready and had spoken before he was.

He could call Celia. He would call Celia. That part was manageable. But the calling wouldn't fix what the last ten minutes had clarified about himself: that saying *yes* in the dark and keeping saying it in the daylight were going to require different things from him. That the daylight was going to keep arriving — full of corridor conversations and Marcus Pryors — and he had assumed, without examining the assumption, that he would always be ready.

He had not been ready today.

He stayed with that for a while. Not the version that spiralled — just the plain fact of it, sitting in the room where he'd placed the light.

Then he called Celia.

She was apologetic immediately. Excited, she said. Should have been more careful. Nothing further until the announcement, whenever Maya was ready. She was sorry.

"It's all right," he said. "She makes the announcement. Not us. On her terms."

"Of course. Completely."

He hung up. That was managed. A small thing handled correctly, one step behind where it should have been.

He finished the calibration, gathered his bag, and walked out into the October afternoon.

He had said: *I chose you, and I've kept choosing you, and I don't think that's going to change.* He'd meant it. Still meant it. But there was a gap between meaning it and living inside it that he hadn't fully mapped, and today had been the first corridor in that gap. There would be others. He was not always going to be the right version of himself in all of them. He understood that now.

He texted Maya on the way home. Not about any of it — just: *Still working late? I have leftover soup that would be embarrassed to be eaten alone.*

Her reply came in under a minute. *Easily manipulated by soup guilt. Give me an hour.*

He put his phone in his pocket and walked on through the city, and thought that being certain and being ready were not the same thing, and that the distance between them was exactly the distance he was going to have to keep crossing.

One corridor conversation at a time.
