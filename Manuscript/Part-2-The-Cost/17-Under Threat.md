# Chapter 17: Under Threat

She didn't go home.

She had meant to — had said goodnight to Ethan in the car and got out at her street and stood on the pavement outside her building for a full minute before she took out her phone and texted him. *Still there?* He replied in under a minute. *Yes. Come back.*

She walked the six blocks. The city was quiet now, the late-night version of itself, the restaurants closing and the last of the theatre crowds dispersed. Her footsteps were loud on the wet pavement. She had been holding something at an even distance for four hours and she could feel it getting closer.

He opened the door before she knocked — again. She had asked him once how he did that and he had said he could hear the stairwell from his desk, which she still didn't entirely believe but had stopped contesting.

She walked in. He closed the door. She stood in the middle of his living room in her coat and didn't take it off.

"He's going to destroy us," she said.

She heard how it sounded as she said it — the particular flatness of a fear that had been managed for several hours and had finally stopped being manageable. Not a question. Not even quite a statement. Just the thing that had been in the room all evening, finally named.

Ethan didn't move toward her. He sat on the edge of the couch and looked at her steadily.

"He only has power if we let him," he said.

"He has a platform. He has readers. He knows people who—"

"I know what he has."

She stopped. He was quiet for a moment, and when he spoke again his voice was the same — even, unhurried, not managing her.

"He can write whatever he writes. He can say whatever he says. The question is whether you get there first."

She looked at him. "What do you mean."

"You control what you reveal and when. He controls it if you stay silent." He leaned forward slightly, elbows on his knees. "The only counter that works is the truth made public on your terms before he can frame it."

She sat down — the armchair across from him, not the couch. Still in her coat. The room was warm and quiet and she was aware of her own breathing.

"What I said to him tonight," she said. "That I'd tell people we were intimate." She looked at her hands. "I don't know if that was brave or desperate."

"It was both," he said. "And it was right."

She looked up.

"He needed to know you're not defenceless," Ethan said. "You're not. You never were. You just didn't know what you were holding yet."

She sat with that for a moment. The coat was too warm but she didn't take it off.

"A second exhibition," he said. "Your work — not the gallery's acquisitions, not a curation. Yours. Your photographs. What you see. Who you are. All of it visible, on your own terms, before he can put his frame around it."

She stared at him. "You want me to—"

"Show yourself first. He can't weaponise what's already public." A pause. "And you've already chosen to. You told Bowen the thesis was about yourself. You gave that talk in November and people cried. You've already been stepping out from behind the work. This is just — doing it all the way."

The room was quiet. Outside the city made its small late sounds.

She took her coat off finally and set it over the arm of the chair.

"Have you thought about what people will think of you?" she said. "Not me — you."

He was very still.

"When this comes out. When people know who I am, what I am. They'll have opinions about you too. About what it says about you that you chose me." She looked at him directly. "I need to know you've actually thought about it. Not just in the abstract. Not just in a good moment. Have you sat with the version where people are unkind, where someone says something at a show, where Jake gets a question he doesn't know how to answer? Have you thought about that version?"

The silence went on long enough that she knew he had.

"Yes," he said.

One word. She waited for the rest of it.

He opened his mouth. Closed it. He looked at the window, then back at her, and she could see him reaching for something and not quite getting hold of it.

"I've—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a version of this where someone at a show says something. Where Jake gets asked something he doesn't have an answer for. Where your name comes up in a way that makes people look at me differently." He was not delivering this cleanly and he seemed to know it — his jaw tightened slightly. "I've sat in that version. I've been in that room in my head. And I—" He exhaled. "I don't have a speech for this. I had one and it's gone."

She looked at him.

"I just — I chose you," he said. "That's it. That's all I've got. I chose you and I've kept choosing you and I don't think that's going to change because some journalist decides to make it his business."

It was not eloquent. It was not what she had half-hoped for — some complete, perfectly weighted declaration that she could hold onto when things got bad. It was a man sitting on a couch in the middle of the night slightly failing to say what he meant.

It was the most believable thing he had ever said to her.

Her throat tightened.

"I need you to mean that," she said.

"I do." He looked at her directly. "That's the one part I'm certain of."

---

She stayed.

Not because they planned anything or decided anything. She just didn't get up to leave when the hour got late and he didn't suggest she should and the city went fully quiet outside and at some point he made tea she didn't drink and they sat in the warm room and the second exhibition became real between them.

"The portraits," he said. He said it carefully, the way he said things that mattered. "I have the work from every session. From the beginning. The ones you know about and the ones I've made since, whenever you've been in a room and the light was right and you weren't watching me watch you."

She looked at him.

"I've never shown them," he said. "I wouldn't. Not without you. But Maya — the work is there. It exists. And it's the truest thing I've made."

She understood what he was saying. A show of his portraits of her — her body, her face, her complete self, everything — in the same gallery where Bret had just stood with his press lanyard and his threat. The people who mattered to her career in that room. Not hidden. Not managed. Fully seen.

"If you show those," she said slowly, "everyone will know what I am."

"Yes."

"Everyone who reads his publications. Everyone Bowen knows. Everyone who might review your work, or mine, or the gallery's."

"Yes."

She sat with the full weight of it. Not the abstract version — the specific one.

People from her undergraduate program. Colleagues who had seen her give papers and respected her work and did not know what she was. People she had been to school with, who had known her at fifteen and sixteen, before she knew what the diagnosis meant or how to carry it. People who would look at a photograph of her body and not have a framework for it and would reach for the nearest available word.

She knew the word. Bret had said it to her in a kitchen three years ago and she had walked forty-five minutes home and sat on the floor of her bathroom and she had never fully gotten out of that bathroom. Some part of her had been in it ever since.

If she put those photographs on a wall, strangers would say it. People who had once meant something to her would say it or think it and not say it, which was sometimes worse. It would be written somewhere, said at a dinner table, repeated. She could not control that. She could not un-ring it. The moment the gallery opened she would be permanently, publicly visible, and some of the people who looked would flinch, and she would know they had, and she would have to keep going anyway.

She sat with all of that. The specific version. The actual faces.

A year ago — two years ago — she would have stopped there. The word, the specific people, the morning after when the world knew. That would have been the end of the conversation. She would have said no before the question was fully asked, because the fear was the whole answer and there was nothing on the other side of it large enough to outweigh it.

What was different now was sitting three feet away from her on the same couch.

Not different in the abstract. Not the idea of love, or the possibility of acceptance, or the theoretical existence of someone who would not flinch. Ethan, specifically. Who had not been told — who had found out through his own hands, without warning, without her having chosen the moment or the words, without any of the careful architecture she had built around every previous telling. He had simply found her, in the dark, and his first response had not been a word at all. It had been staying. It had been his hands not moving away.

She had told Bret. She had chosen him, trusted him, given him the information deliberately, and he had used the word. Ethan had not been given anything. He had discovered her, and he had stayed, and he had not used any word that diminished her, not once, not in all the months since. She had been looking for the flinch since that first night and it had never come.

That was what had moved. Not her courage — she was not sure she was braver than she had been at twenty-three. The ground had moved. She had proof now that the word was not inevitable. That someone could know everything and stay. That the worst thing Bret had told her about herself was simply not true.

She could not have walked into that gallery carrying only herself. She was walking in carrying that proof.

Then she sat with the other thing.

"You'd be showing them as your work," she said. "Your name on it."

"It is my work." He met her eyes. "And yours. You'd choose every image. Nothing goes on that wall that you haven't stood in front of and said yes to. You control what's shown and how. That's the whole point — it has to be yours as much as mine."

The room was quiet. She could hear the city outside, the occasional car, the night settling into itself.

She thought about what Bret had in his notebook. What he could write. The frame he could put around her life, her body, her secret — words in print, in the publications that reached the people she needed to take her seriously. She thought about how a story looks when someone else tells it first.

And then she thought about a room full of Ethan's photographs. Her face. Her body. Her truth on the walls in careful light, chosen by the two of them together, presented on their own terms before Bret could open his mouth.

Some people would still use the word. She knew that. She was going in knowing that. But at least the story would be hers.

"When?" she said.

He didn't let the relief show — she knew him well enough to see him not showing it, which was its own thing. "Celia has a spring slot. I'd need to ask. But it's possible."

"Ask her."

It was nearly two. Neither of them moved to end the evening.

At some point she went to the bathroom and came back and he had turned off the main light, just the lamp now, and she stood in the doorway looking at him and the question of going home didn't arise. It hadn't arisen for a while. She wasn't sure exactly when his house had stopped being a place she visited and started being a place she returned to, but it had — the way all the real changes had happened, quietly and without announcement, until she looked up and found herself already on the other side.

She got into bed. He came in after, turned the lamp off, and she felt the warmth of him settling beside her and then his arm coming around her from behind, not holding on too tight, just there. Present. The way he was always present — without requirement, without performance.

She lay in the dark and listened to the city outside going fully quiet.

The fear was still there. It would be there tomorrow and when Celia confirmed the spring slot and when the first print went on the wall. She wasn't going to pretend otherwise. But it shared the room now with something solid — the knowledge that she had spoken tonight and not been destroyed by it, and that the person behind her had said *yes* having thought about the hard version, and that his photographs of her existed in the world, in flat files, waiting.

His weapon only worked if she was still hiding.

She wasn't hiding.

She closed her eyes. His breathing slowed beside her. Outside, the last sounds of the city thinned to nothing, and she lay in the quiet and let herself be exactly where she was.
