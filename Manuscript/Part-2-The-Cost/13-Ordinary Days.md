# Chapter 13: Ordinary Days

November arrived without ceremony and the city adjusted — heavier coats, earlier dark, the particular quality of light through cloud that made everything look considered. Maya adjusted with it.

She noticed she was adjusting. That was new.

The mornings had a shape now that hadn't been there before. Coffee before she was fully awake. Ethan's texts arriving somewhere between seven and seven-thirty — nothing significant, a photograph he'd taken walking in, a question about her evening, once a single sentence about a Hartley canvas that had made her put her mug down and reply for twenty minutes. She had started to look for the notification the way she looked for the kettle on a cold morning. Not urgently. Just — expected. Good.

She stayed later at the museum. Not because there was more work, but because she liked being in the galleries in the low afternoon light with the public thinning and the rooms going quiet. She would walk the permanent collection the way she used to walk it at night, except now she wasn't avoiding anything. She was just looking.

She laughed more. She noticed this too — that the laugh came easier, that it arrived before she'd decided to let it, that she stopped editing it back to something smaller. Zoe noticed. She didn't say anything, which was more pointed than if she had.

---

Jake noticed on a Tuesday evening in the pub — the three of them, a corner table, rain against the windows. Ethan had gone to the bar and Jake leaned across the table with the directness that Maya had come to understand was just how he was.

"Whatever happened," he said, "it worked."

She looked at him. "What do you mean?"

"With him." He nodded toward the bar. "He used to come to these things and be — present but somewhere else. Like the best part of him was being saved for later." He picked up his glass. "It's not being saved anymore."

She looked at Ethan at the bar — his back to them, easy in the room, saying something to the barman that made the barman laugh.

"He's like that with me too," she said.

"I know." Jake set his glass down. "That's what I mean."

Ethan came back with three drinks and set them down and looked at the two of them.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing," Jake said. "We were talking about you."

"Reassuring."

"Entirely positive," Maya said.

He sat down and they argued cheerfully about a film for forty minutes and resolved nothing, and Maya sat in the warm pub with the rain outside and her glass in her hand and thought: *this is what ordinary looks like. I didn't know.*

---

On a Thursday evening she cooked dinner at his house.

This had not been a planned thing. She had been on her way home from campus with the intention of working on the appendix and had texted him about something else entirely and the conversation had drifted, the way their conversations drifted, and by the time she was getting off the subway she was going the wrong direction.

He opened the door before she knocked — he had heard her in the stairwell, he said, which she didn't entirely believe — and she walked in and stood in his kitchen and opened his refrigerator and assessed.

"You have eggs and half a red onion and something in foil that I'm not going to investigate," she said.

"There's pasta in the cupboard above the stove."

"Then go away. I'll figure it out."

He retreated to the kitchen table and opened his sketchpad with the exaggerated compliance of someone who had been told to sit down and was sitting down very deliberately.

"You could at least look grateful," she said, without turning around.

"I am grateful. Silently."

"Silently isn't a flavour."

"I'm grateful in a rich, complex way that doesn't require vocalisation."

She glanced back at him over her shoulder. He was looking at his sketchpad with perfect innocence.

"You're going to eat whatever I make," she said, "and you're going to say it's good."

"I was going to say that anyway. I have no standards when someone else is cooking."

"That's either very easy or very low."

"Both," he said. "Comfortably both."

She turned back to the stove. She was smiling and she didn't bother hiding it because he couldn't see her face.

He sat at the kitchen table with his sketchpad and she cooked the way she cooked when she wasn't performing it — efficiently, without ceremony, the radio on low, moving around his kitchen with the ease of someone who had been doing it for months. She had been doing it for months. Somewhere between the sixth and seventh visit she had stopped thinking of it as his kitchen and started thinking of it as the kitchen, which was a small thing and not a small thing.

He didn't offer to help. He had learned she didn't want help while cooking — she had told him once, precisely, that cooking was the one activity that required exactly the amount of brain it took and therefore left no room for conversation, and he had filed it and never offered again. She had not had to say it twice. She was still getting used to that.

They ate at the kitchen table with the radio on and the rain coming in waves against the window. She had made something out of eggs and pasta and the onion and herbs from the small pot on his windowsill that she had given him in September and which he had, against all expectation, kept alive. He ate without commenting on the food, which was how she preferred it. When the plates were empty he made tea without asking and she read the paper and he went back to his sketchpad and the rain kept coming and neither of them said anything for a long time and it was the most comfortable Maya had been in any room in years.

"I've been thinking about the appendix," she said eventually.

"The Flemish painter."

"How did you—"

"You mentioned it Tuesday. The one whose early work doesn't fit the argument but whose late work does."

She looked at him over the paper. "I wasn't sure you were listening."

"I'm always listening." He made a mark on the page. "You said you weren't sure whether to include work that didn't fit or just the work that did."

"I'm going to include the early work," she said. "The part that doesn't fit is the honest part. The argument is stronger if I show where it breaks."

He looked up. "Yes," he said. "Exactly."

She went back to the paper. He went back to the sketchpad. The rain eased.

After a while he said: "When does Bowen want the final draft?"

"End of January."

"That's six weeks."

"I know how many weeks it is, Ethan."

He looked up. Something in her voice had shifted — not sharp, but closed off, the particular flatness that arrived when she felt managed.

"I wasn't—" he started.

"I know you weren't. It's fine." She turned a page.

He set the pencil down. "You do that sometimes."

"Do what."

"Shut a conversation down before it becomes one." He said it evenly, not accusatory. "I was going to say six weeks is enough time, not that it isn't."

She looked at him. Her jaw was tight.

"I've had people manage my timeline on this thesis for two years," she said. "Bowen, the department, my committee. Every single one of them with an opinion about how long things should take."

"I'm not them."

"I know that."

"But I sound like them when I ask about a deadline."

She was quiet for a moment. The rain had stopped. The kitchen was very still.

"Yes," she said. "Sometimes."

He picked up the pencil. Made a mark that wasn't really a mark. "Okay," he said. "Then I won't ask about deadlines."

"You can ask." She turned back to the paper. "Just — give me a second when I'm being unreasonable."

"You weren't being unreasonable."

"I was a little bit."

"A little bit," he agreed.

She turned a page without reading it.

She looked at him across the table. His head was down, the sketchpad open, the pencil moving in the loose, not-quite-drawing way it did when he was thinking about something else.

She thought: *the thesis isn't about the artists. It never was. It was about me. And I have been carrying that in my hands for two years and I could put it down right now.*

She opened her mouth.

The pencil stopped. He didn't look up — but she felt the change in the room, the way the quality of his stillness shifted when he was listening for something.

She looked at the paper in her hands. A photograph of a Flemish painter she had never met, dead for four hundred years, whose early work didn't fit the argument.

"Nothing," she said. "Sorry."

He looked up then. Held the look for a moment. Then went back to the sketchpad. No question. No press. Just the room continuing, and him in it, available.

She stayed until nearly eleven, which was later than she'd meant to, and walked home through the wet streets with the unsaid sentence still in her chest.

---

She was in bed when she opened the bookmarks folder.

She hadn't planned to. She had been reading something else — an article about a Flemish painter she was considering for the thesis appendix — and then somehow she was in the folder named *maybe* and the theologian's piece was open on her screen.

She had read it three times the night she saved it. She read it again now.

The argument was careful and specific. Not sentimental, not advocacy — just theology, working through the question of bodies that didn't sort into the categories the tradition had assumed. The scholar cited Scripture the way her grandmother had read it — not as a weapon or a proof, but as a place to stand. *Fearfully and wonderfully made* meant something specific, he argued. It meant: before the body was a problem to be solved, it was a gift being given. The wonder came first.

She read that sentence twice.

She thought about the note in the drawer. The Post-it in her own careful sixteen-year-old handwriting, the adhesive long gone, carried forward through every move in a box she never labelled but always knew where to find.

She closed the article. Opened it again. Saved it to a second folder — one she did not name *maybe*.

She didn't know what to call it. She left it unnamed.

She put her phone on the nightstand and lay in the dark for a while. The city was quiet outside. Ethan would be asleep by now — he was an early-to-bed person, which she had found out early and which she found, inexplicably, endearing. She thought about texting him goodnight and decided against it, because he was asleep, and because the thing she was carrying right now was hers to carry for a little while longer before she put it into words.

She lay in the dark and let it be what it was.

She didn't pray. But she thought about what it would mean to.
