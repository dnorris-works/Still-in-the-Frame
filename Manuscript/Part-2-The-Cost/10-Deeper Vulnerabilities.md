# Chapter 10: Deeper Vulnerabilities

She cancelled on a Wednesday, which she never did.

They'd had plans — coffee at the museum before her morning in the archive, something they'd fallen into doing most Wednesdays. Ethan found out via text at seven-fifteen: a brief, uncharacteristically flat message that said she wasn't coming and not to worry about it. He read it twice, noted the absence of any of her usual precision, and was at the pharmacy by seven forty-five.

She didn't answer the door immediately. He knocked again, heard movement, and then her voice through the door: "I said don't worry about it."

"I brought soup."

A pause.

"From where?"

"The place on Clement. The one you mentioned."

Another pause, longer. The lock turned.

She looked terrible. Her hair was loose and unwashed, pulled back unevenly. She was wearing an oversized sweatshirt and no socks and had the particular hollowness around the eyes of someone who hadn't slept well. She looked at the bag in his hand, then at him.

"You didn't have to do that."

"I know." He held up the bag. "Can I come in?"

She stepped back.

The apartment was dim, curtains half-drawn, the particular quiet of a space accommodating illness. He found the kitchen, ladled soup into a bowl he located after one wrong cupboard, and brought it to the couch where she'd resettled herself under a blanket.

"Eat," he said.

"I'm not hungry."

"Then sit with it until you are."

She looked at him over the bowl with an expression that was too tired to be properly irritated. He sat in the armchair across from her and picked up the book on the coffee table — a survey of twentieth-century American photography, heavily annotated in her handwriting. He opened it to a random page.

"Do you want me to read?"

"You don't have to stay."

"Maya."

He read aloud from where the book fell open — a passage about Dorothea Lange and the particular ethical weight of documentary photography, the responsibility of the person holding the camera. He had opinions about it. He kept them to himself.

She ate half the soup without noticing she was doing it.

After a while Zoe came out of her room, stopped in the doorway, and took in the scene — Ethan in the armchair with the book, Maya bundled on the couch, the empty half-bowl on the coffee table. She looked at him with an expression he couldn't fully read.

"There's more soup," he said. "In the pot."

"I ate already." Zoe leaned against the door frame. "She hates being looked after."

"She'll pretend to be fine in about an hour and try to send you home."

"I'll pretend to believe her and stay anyway."

Zoe looked at him for another moment. Then she nodded once — a small, decided movement — and went back to her room.

He kept reading.

Maya fell asleep somewhere in a discussion of light metering and the ethics of candid portraiture. He set the book down carefully and looked at her — her face completely unguarded in sleep, the lines she usually held in her expression gone, something younger and less defended underneath.

He thought about the night at his apartment. What she'd shown him. The way she'd stood there with her whole self exposed and her face completely open, waiting.

What had happened between them was still in the developer. It required care.

He got up quietly and washed the bowl.

When she woke an hour later, blinking and disoriented, he was back in the chair with a different book.

"You're still here," she said.

"I told you I would be."

She looked at him with an expression that was hard to name — something open and unguarded that she hadn't quite managed to close before he saw it.

"I haven't let anyone take care of me in years," she said. Quietly.

"I know," he said. "You don't have to do anything about that right now."

She pulled the blanket tighter and looked at the middle distance and didn't say anything else. But she didn't ask him to leave either, which he understood was its own kind of answer.

He stayed until evening. She slept again for an hour and woke to find him in the kitchen making tea, unhurried, at home in her space in a way that should have felt intrusive and didn't. She watched him from the doorway for a moment before he noticed her. He just held up the second mug. She crossed the kitchen and took it.

Later, walking home through the dark city, he thought about the two things he'd seen in her that day: the resistance and the release. The instinct to send him away and the thing underneath it that had wanted him to stay. He wasn't sure you could photograph that. But he thought about trying.

---

Three weeks later, she was recovered and the weather had turned and he asked her on a Thursday, casually, in the museum courtyard over lunch.

"I want to photograph you properly. Not candid shots — a portrait series. Different locations, natural light." He watched her face. "Only if you want to."

She thought about it. "Why me?"

"Because you're interesting to look at."

"That's not a reason."

"It's the only honest reason I have." He picked up his coffee. "The rest of it is technical — the way light moves across your face, the way you look at things. But that's not why. The why is just that I want to."

She looked at him for a long moment. "Okay," she said.

She regretted it on Saturday morning, standing on the steps of the public library in the low October light while he loaded film and checked his settings with the focused quiet of a person entering a different mode.

"I don't know what to do with my face," she said.

"Don't do anything with it." He raised the camera. "Look at the building."

She looked at the building.

"What do you see?"

"Civic optimism. 1917. Someone believed the public deserved beauty." She tilted her head. "The cornices are slightly uneven on the east end. The stone was sourced from two different quarries and they didn't match as well as the architect hoped."

The shutter clicked.

She turned. "Were you—"

"Yes." He lowered the camera. "Don't look at me like that, that was a good one."

She made herself not smile and mostly failed.

They moved through four locations across the morning — the library steps, a stretch of wrought-iron fence along the park, the narrow alley behind the museum where the light came down at noon in a single concentrated shaft, and finally the waterfront, where the wind made everything difficult and the results were, he said, going to be interesting.

She stopped being self-conscious somewhere around the second location. He'd ask her to look at something, or think about something, or simply stand and let the light do what it did, and then the shutter would click and she'd feel nothing except faintly surprised.

"You're not shooting the way I expected," she said, at the waterfront.

"How did you expect?"

"More direction. More 'chin up, turn left.'"

"That's not what I'm doing." He advanced the film. "I'm not trying to make you look a certain way. I'm trying to catch you looking like yourself."

She stood in the wind off the water and thought about that.

"Ethan."

"Yeah."

"You make me feel beautiful."

He lowered the camera and looked at her directly — not through the lens, just himself, his actual eyes. The wind moved her hair across her face and she didn't push it back.

"You are beautiful," he said. "I'm just pointing that out."

She looked away, at the water, because the directness of it was almost too much to hold.

He raised the camera again. The shutter clicked.

---

He developed the portraits alone on a Sunday night.

The apartment was quiet, the city doing its late-Sunday thing outside, and he worked through the process methodically — developer, stop, fixer, wash — his hands moving in the dark with the automatic ease of long practice while his mind did something else.

The images came up one by one in the tray.

He'd taken forty-three frames across the four locations. From those, eleven were what he'd hoped for. From those eleven, three were something more.

He clipped them to the wire and stood back.

He stood there for a long time.

The library image: her profile sharp against the stone, her expression unmistakably that of someone thinking about something that mattered. The alley: light falling across her at the angle he'd calculated, her eyes down, a quality of inwardness that made the image feel like an intrusion and a gift simultaneously. The waterfront: wind in her hair, looking away at the water, the exact moment before she'd composed herself back into place. Something visible in that half-second that she hadn't meant to show.

His mother's voice, which he still heard sometimes when he was working: *Does she know? Does she consent?*

He thought about exhibiting them. He thought about the group show coming up in March, the open call he'd been considering submitting to. These images were the best work he'd done in years. Maybe the best he'd ever done.

He thought about Maya's face on the couch when he'd found the museum photo — that brief unguarded moment of being caught, being seen without having chosen it.

He took out his notebook and wrote: *Talk to Maya before anything. Her image, her decision. Ask, don't present.*

He looked at the three prints again.

He wasn't capturing her. He understood that now, standing in the red light with the chemical smell around him and her face looking back at him from three angles. He was being trusted with something. The question was whether he was worthy of it, and the only way to answer that was to ask.

He turned off the darkroom light and went to make tea and thought about how to begin that conversation.
