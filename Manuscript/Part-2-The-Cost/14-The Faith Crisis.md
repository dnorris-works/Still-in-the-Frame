# Chapter 14: The Faith Crisis

It happened on a Wednesday, which felt wrong — like a crisis should at least have the decency to arrive on a significant day.

She was walking home from campus. The thesis meeting had gone well; Bowen had liked the new direction of the second chapter and had used the word *rigorous*, which from Bowen was essentially a standing ovation. Maya had her bag over one shoulder and her notes in her hand and she was thinking about the Voss argument and whether the section on concealment as protection needed to come before or after the section on concealment as cost.

She turned the corner onto Hargreaves Street and stopped.

Riverside Community Church sat at the end of the block, the way it always had, the way it had the night she met Ethan — warm light through the tall windows, the dark stone of the façade, the wooden noticeboard with its hand-lettered weekly schedule. On Wednesday evenings, she now knew, there was a mid-week service. Seven o'clock. She had walked past it before without stopping.

Tonight she stopped.

Through the windows she could see people. Standing, then sitting. She was too far away to hear the words but she could hear the shape of it — voices together, the low swell of something being sung. Not performed. Just — offered. The particular quality of a room full of people who believed they belonged there.

She stood on the pavement and looked at it for a long time.

She had been inside a church three times as an adult. A wedding — her cousin Diane's, all white flowers and careful lighting. Her grandmother's funeral, six years ago. And once at sixteen, the last time she'd gone voluntarily — a Sunday morning, third pew from the front, sitting beside her grandmother and feeling, briefly, the thing her grandmother always seemed to feel. Something solid. Something that didn't need explaining.

She had stopped going after that. Not dramatically. Gradually, then completely.

---

*If God made me this way*, she thought, standing on the pavement outside Riverside Community Church on a Wednesday evening, *why does the church say I'm wrong?*

She thought of a pamphlet someone had left in the university library once, years ago — not for her, just left there. She had read three pages in a bathroom stall and then folded it very carefully and put it in the bin. She could still see the font. The word *disordered* in plain serif type, as though it were simply a fact, the way boiling point was a fact, the way gravity was.

She had found the other piece a year later. The one she had found late one night when she had typed *intersex and Christianity* into a search bar and mostly hated what came back. One theologian, buried in the results, had said something different. She had written the name down and looked it up again twice since. She hadn't told anyone about that. It lived in a bookmarks folder she had named simply *maybe*.

But she had read the other answers too. The careful ones. The ones that were not cruel — that wanted to be kind, she thought, that were trying — and had still arrived at the same place. *You must choose.* As if she could amputate half of what she was and call it obedience. As if the choosing itself was not a kind of violence. She had read a bishop's letter once, thoughtful, measured, full of words like *pastoral* and *accompaniment*, that concluded she was called to celibacy as a gift from God. She had sat with that for an hour. She had understood what it was trying to do. And then she had understood what it was actually doing, which was telling her that the only permissible response to being what she was, was to be nothing at all.

She had XX chromosomes and XY chromosomes. Both. Not metaphorically. Not as identity or aspiration. As cellular fact, as biological reality, as the literal truth of every tissue in her body. She was male and female in the same body, at the same time, without remainder.

The church said: marry the opposite sex.

She stood on the pavement and held that.

She had no opposite sex. She *was* the opposite sex. Whatever she married would be, by some reading, the same — and by another reading, different. The category didn't hold. It had never been designed for a person like her, which meant it had never been designed for her at all, which meant every answer the institution offered was an answer to a question it had mistaken for hers.

This was not bitterness. She had moved past bitterness years ago, the way you moved past a town that had nothing for you — not with anger, just with the understanding that you were going somewhere else. This was something colder and more precise: the recognition that the institution was not being cruel to her specifically. It simply had no category that fit. And in the absence of a category, it had offered her the nearest available thing, which was silence, or celibacy, or the instruction to choose — and none of those were answers. They were the shape of an answer with nothing inside.

She thought about her grandmother. Who had held a green Bible and read *fearfully and wonderfully made* with complete certainty, as if it were simply true, as if it had always been true, as if no institution's confusion about categories could alter the fact of it. Her grandmother had not been a theologian. She had not argued. She had simply believed, with the quiet totality of someone who had decided once and never needed to decide again.

Her grandmother had died when Maya was seventeen — before the school library, before the word, before any of it. She had never known. And Maya had never had to watch her find out.

*Maybe that's all faith is*, Maya thought. *Deciding that the verse is still true regardless.*

The singing inside rose slightly, then settled.

She took three steps toward the door.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with a kind of detached curiosity — the shaking was physical, a body-response she hadn't ordered, her hands simply reporting what the rest of her already knew. That she was afraid. That the fear was not of the building but of what might be inside it. Not rejection — she had survived rejection, she knew its shape. What she was afraid of was the other thing. The possibility that she walked in and it was real, and it had room for her, and she had been wrong to stay away.

She put her hand on the iron handle.

The metal was cold. Through the door she could feel the faint warmth of the room beyond, the collective body heat of people gathered, the muffled sound of the service continuing without her.

She stood there for a full minute. Maybe longer.

Then she let go of the handle, stepped back, and walked away.

---

She walked fast for the first two blocks. Just walking with purpose. Just going home.

By the third block she slowed.

By the fourth she had stopped in front of a newsagent's window, staring at a display of greeting cards without seeing them, her bag heavy on her shoulder, her notes still in her hand.

Not that she believed the church would reject her. She had prepared for that. She had a whole internal architecture built for handling rejection — she had been building it since she was sixteen, since the school library, since Bret's kitchen. She could absorb rejection. She had done it before.

What she couldn't absorb was the possibility that she walked in and *it was good*, and then it found out what she was, and closed.

Twice, now, she had opened a door and had it shut in her face. Her mother's silence. Bret. She could not do it a third time with God.

Better to stay outside. Better to keep the door closed yourself than have it closed on you.

She started walking again.

---

Her apartment was cold when she got in — she'd left the window cracked and the evening had turned. Zoe's coat and keys were gone from the hook by the door; she was at Marcus's, probably until morning. Maya was glad of it. She turned the lamp on. Put the kettle on. Stood in the kitchen waiting for it to boil, her coat still on, her mind doing the thing it did when she was unsettled: moving fast over the surface of things, not landing.

She was looking for her phone charger in the drawer beside her bed when she found it.

A Post-it note, pale yellow, the adhesive long since dried to nothing. She had stuck it to the inside of the drawer at some point and forgotten it — or not forgotten, exactly, but let it sit there at the back of the drawer where she didn't have to see it unless she was looking.

The handwriting was her own but younger — neater, more careful, the handwriting of someone who was still practising. She had copied it at sixteen, she remembered. The summer before everything changed. Her grandmother had read it to her from a small worn Bible with a green cover and Maya had asked for a pen and paper because she wanted to keep it.

*I praise you because I am fearfully and wonderfully made; your works are wonderful, I know that full well.*

Psalm 139. The same psalm. The piece in the east corner of the gallery.

She stood very still in her bedroom, the Post-it note between her fingers, the kettle beginning to whistle in the kitchen.

She had written this down at sixteen because it had felt true. Because her grandmother had read it with such complete certainty — not performing certainty, just *having* it, the way she had everything, like a piece of furniture, solid — and Maya had wanted to hold onto that. Before the school library. Before the word and what it meant and the understanding that there was no map for people like her, not in the world she'd been handed.

She had kept it for twelve years — through two moves, a student hall, this apartment. Every time she packed a drawer she had found it and put it in the next box without quite deciding to. Not thrown it away. Not framed it. Just carried it forward, in the dark, not quite able to let it go.

She read it again.

*I am fearfully and wonderfully made.*

She thought about the artist who had believed that. The collage in the east corner, the figure in the doorway, the fragments of scripture layered over everything. Someone who had stood where Maya stood and arrived at a different conclusion.

She thought about Ethan: *Maybe God knew you needed something Bret couldn't give you.*

She thought about her grandmother's green Bible and the way she had read from it — not as argument, not as proof, just as fact. The way you read the weather.

She put the Post-it note back in the drawer. She didn't throw it away. She had never thrown it away.

She went to deal with the kettle.

She had stood outside it tonight with her hand on the handle, and the world had not ended, and the note was still in the drawer, and that — for now — was enough.
