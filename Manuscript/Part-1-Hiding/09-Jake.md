# Chapter 9: Jake

He asked if they could meet for coffee.

Not a pub — coffee, which was not Jake's usual register, and which Maya understood immediately as an attempt to signal that this was different from the ordinary kind of meeting. He sent the text to her directly, not through Ethan, which she understood as a second signal. He suggested a café near her campus, which was out of his way, which she understood as a third.

She said yes. She told Ethan she was going, and he nodded without asking what she expected, which was the right response.

---

Jake was already there when she arrived. He had also chosen a table away from the window — not conspicuously, but deliberately. He stood when he saw her, which was not something he ordinarily did, and something in the gesture made her tired and sorry for him simultaneously.

"Thanks for coming," he said.

"Of course." She sat. He sat.

He ordered for both of them without asking, which would have annoyed her under other circumstances and didn't now, because he'd remembered: milk, a little sugar. She noted it. He'd been paying attention, in his way, for a long time.

They sat for a moment with the coffee between them. Jake had the look of someone who had rehearsed something and had now forgotten it, which she suspected was more honest than whatever he'd planned to say.

"I know I sent a card," he said.

"I got it."

"It was — I meant what was in it. I just also know that a card isn't—" He stopped. His hands were around his mug. "I've been trying to find the right moment for this for three weeks and there isn't one, so."

She waited.

"What I said to Ethan, after — after he told me. About you." He met her eyes briefly and then looked at his mug. "I thought I was being a good friend. Asking him to examine himself. Making sure he wasn't going to hurt you by not knowing what he was." He pressed his lips together. "What I was actually doing was putting my discomfort into language that sounded like care. I didn't know what to do with what he'd told me, and I handed that to him as if it were his problem to solve. And he took it seriously, because he's him, and he sat inside it all day instead of—"

"I know what that silence looked like," Maya said.

"Yes." He said it quietly. "I know you do."

The café was doing its ordinary Friday-morning thing around them. Someone's laptop. A pushchair. The coffee machine making its noise.

"I'm not a bad person," Jake said. "I want to say that, not as a defence — I know it's not a defence — but because I think you should know that I know the difference between *not a bad person* and *did a bad thing*. I did a bad thing. I caused harm to someone I love, through someone I also love, because I didn't have the vocabulary for what I was hearing and I improvised badly."

Maya looked at him.

He was not performing remorse. He was just sitting there with it, the way Ethan sat with things — inherited quality, she supposed, or chosen one. He hadn't dressed it up or looked for absolution. He'd just put it on the table.

"You said *bisexual*," she said.

"I know."

"You said it like it would explain him. Like if he was bisexual then what happened between him and me was about his sexuality, not about me."

"Yes." His jaw tightened slightly. "That was wrong."

"It was also — it wasn't about his sexuality. It was about mine." She held his eyes now. "My body was the new information. And you made it about a question he should answer about himself, which made it not about me at all. Which is what people do, I've found. When they don't know what to do with me, they redirect to how this reflects on them. Or on him."

Jake was very still.

"I was trying to protect him," he said. "That's what I thought I was doing. I wasn't trying to make it about me, or about — I was trying to make sure he didn't hurt you by not knowing what he was."

"I understand that."

"Then you understand I wasn't being malicious."

"I understand you weren't being malicious," she said. "That's not the same as understanding that what you did was all right."

He looked at her. A flash of something — not quite defensiveness, but close. The look of someone who had rehearsed an apology and had not rehearsed the part where the apology wasn't enough.

"I know it caused harm," he said.

"It wasn't just harm." Her voice was even. "You took the question of what happened between us and made it a question about him. About his identity. Which meant I stopped being a person in the room and became a — a category. A thing he needed to figure out his relationship to." She paused. "That's what people do when they don't know what to do with me. And I'm used to it. That doesn't make it right."

The flash was gone. He looked at his mug. His jaw moved. She could see him arriving somewhere he hadn't been before — not performing the work, actually doing it.

"No," he said. After a moment. "It doesn't."

She looked at her coffee. Outside, a cyclist passed. Someone's phone rang and went to voicemail.

"I'm not going to tell you it was fine," she said. "It wasn't. That silence was—" She stopped. Not because she was protecting him but because she didn't owe him the detail of it. "It's in the past. He came back. We're here."

He nodded. His eyes were wet, which she had not seen before and which she looked away from quickly to give him the dignity of it.

"I'm glad," he said. "I am genuinely, completely glad. And I'm sorry. And I wanted to say it to you, not just to him."

She looked at him for a moment.

"Thank you," she said. "For the direct version."

He almost smiled. "Ethan said you'd prefer it."

"Ethan knows me."

"Yes." He picked up his mug. "He really does."

They stayed for another twenty minutes, and talked about other things — Priya's promotion, a film they'd both seen, Rachel's opinions on things in general which Jake confirmed were extensive and largely correct. The air between them was not easy exactly, but it was clear. The thing had been said. Neither of them had to carry it separately anymore.

On the pavement outside he hugged her — briefly, firmly, the hug of someone who had been wanting to do it and had finally found the right moment. She let him.

She walked back to campus with the feeling of something that had been waiting to resolve finally resolving — not with drama, not with a speech, just two people in a café on a Friday morning deciding to stop carrying something alone.

She texted Ethan on the way: *Done. He was good.*

His reply came back in under a minute: *I'm glad.*

She pocketed her phone and kept walking.
