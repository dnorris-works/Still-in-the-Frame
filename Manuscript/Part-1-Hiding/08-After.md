# Chapter 8: After

The pub was Jake's idea, which meant it was louder than necessary and had an excellent jukebox.

It was two days after. Two days since his house, his couch, his arms around her while the world declined to end. One of those days had been silence, and then a doorbell, and then his arms again — tighter this time, the holding of someone who had understood what he nearly lost. She had woken this morning with the particular feeling of someone who has survived something they had not expected to survive and survived it twice.

Zoe knew everything. She'd told her the parts Zoe hadn't seen — the night before, the silence, what she'd sent — and Zoe had filled in the rest herself, having opened the door and then quietly left. She had cried and delivered her verdict on Jake — well-meaning idiot, in terms that required no further elaboration — and that had been that. But Zoe knowing was one thing. This table was another.

She sat across from Jake and Priya in a pub that smelled of old wood and good beer, and Ethan was beside her with his hand resting near hers on the table, and nobody here knew what had happened two nights ago, and she found she didn't mind. She could carry it quietly in this room. It was still hers.

Priya was an environmental lawyer with strong opinions about most things and no patience for anyone who didn't bring their full self to a conversation. Within four minutes she and Maya were deep in something about urban water rights that had left both Jake and Ethan behind.

Jake was being warm. More warm than usual, which Ethan noticed and which Maya did not, and which Jake was probably not even fully aware of — the particular warmth of a person who knows they owe something and is paying it in the currency available.

"She's different," Jake said to Ethan, under the music.

"You said that already."

"I'm saying it again." Jake drank his beer. "Watch her. She's the same at this table as she was when you described her at the museum. A lot of people aren't."

Ethan watched. Jake was right — there was no shift in her when the setting changed, no performance for new people, no version of herself adjusted for the audience. She was asking Priya a question now, leaning forward with her elbows on the table, genuinely wanting the answer. Priya was gesturing widely. Maya laughed at something — the real laugh, the one he'd been cataloguing since the first night — and Priya laughed too and pointed at her like she'd said exactly the right thing.

"Good different," Jake said.

"Yeah."

"Are you going to tell her that?"

"I tell her things."

"Ethan." Jake set his glass down. "I have known you for eight years. I have watched you be perfectly pleasant with perfectly wrong women and perfectly cowardly about ending it each time, because you are, unfortunately, a decent person even when you're being cowardly." A pause. "This is not that. Don't be cowardly about this one."

Ethan looked at Maya. She'd turned back to the table, found his eyes across it, and smiled — not the polite one, the other one.

"I'm not," he said.

Later, when Priya had gone to the bar and Jake had followed to help carry drinks, Maya leaned across the table toward him.

"I like them," she said.

"They like you. Jake's been signalling approval at me from across the table for an hour."

"Is he subtle about it?"

"He pointed at you and gave me a thumbs up while you were looking at your phone."

She laughed. "I want you to meet Zoe."

"I want to meet Zoe."

"She's going to interrogate you."

"Good." He picked up his glass. "I'll pass."

She looked at him steadily. "You probably will," she said.

On the way home she took his hand on the street without any of the deliberateness of the hiking trail. Just reached over and took it. He held on.

---

Rachel texted Jake that night.

*I know what you said to him. I need you to understand something: she has been through things you and I will never fully understand, and she let him in anyway. That took more courage than either of us will ever have to find. You nearly took that from both of them. I'm not going to stay angry about it because I can see you already know. But I needed to say it to you directly.*

Jake read it twice. He didn't reply. He set his phone down and sat with it for a while, which was the only honest response available to him.

---

The thesis presentation was the following Tuesday — four days after the pub, a week after the darkroom, and she had not yet found a way to think about either without losing her train of thought entirely.

The conference room held thirty people and felt full.

Maya stood at the lectern with her notes in front of her and didn't look at them. She'd written the talk six times. She knew it the way she knew the paintings she'd studied — not as information to be retrieved but as something lived in, walked through so many times the rooms were familiar.

She looked at the room instead. Dr. Bowen in the second row, hands folded, watching with that particular expression that meant she was genuinely interested rather than professionally attentive. Three faculty she recognised. A dozen graduate students from her cohort, laptops open, pens ready. And at the back, arrived late and sitting very still so as not to draw attention, Ethan.

She hadn't told him the time. He'd asked Rachel, apparently, who had asked Zoe.

She looked at him for one second. He raised his hand slightly — not a wave, just an acknowledgement. *I'm here.*

She began.

"Hidden Narratives in Abstract Expressionism begins with a question that sounds simple and isn't: what does an artist owe the viewer?" She let that sit for a moment. "We assume transparency. We assume the work is an offering — here is what I made, here is what I meant. But a significant number of the most important works of the twentieth century were built on concealment. Not deception — concealment. The difference matters."

She moved through the argument the way she moved through galleries — without rushing, letting each point establish before the next arrived. The Voss analysis. The technique-as-narrative framework. Three case studies, each one tightening the thesis. She could feel the room's attention, the quality of it changing as the argument built, people leaning forward without knowing they were.

She talked about artists who had hidden personal histories in their brushwork. Who had encoded grief, desire, shame, joy — things too large or too dangerous to say plainly — into the texture of the work itself. How the concealment was not a failure of honesty but a different kind of honesty. A slower kind. The kind that trusted the right person to find it eventually.

Two nights ago, the right person had found it.

She hadn't planned to think about that. The thought arrived unbidden during the Voss analysis, and she let it sit there quietly while her voice kept moving through the argument. The concealment and the artist are inseparable. You cannot understand the work without understanding what the maker had been unable to say. She had written those words two years ago, sitting in a library carrel at midnight, thinking she was writing about paint.

And somewhere underneath the Voss argument, a second thought arrived — the one that had been circling since she first started this thesis, the one she kept setting aside: *this is what I've been doing with God too.* Hiding. Deciding the answer was no before asking the question. Assuming the rejection was certain so she never had to find out.

But she had asked the question, two nights ago. Not in words. In the most exposed way possible. And the answer had not been rejection.

She pushed both thoughts aside and kept talking. But they had been there, and she knew it.

She finished to sustained applause that surprised her, which it always did.

Dr. Bowen caught her eye and nodded once — the nod that meant: *yes. exactly that.*

Ethan was waiting at the back of the room when the faculty finished their questions. He didn't push through to reach her. He waited, which was such a specific Ethan thing to do that it made her chest ache.

When the room had cleared enough she crossed to him.

"You came," she said.

"Did you think I wouldn't?"

"I didn't tell you the time."

"No." He smiled. "You didn't."

She looked at him standing there in his good jacket — this man who had held her without making it into anything, who had asked permission in the quietest voice she'd ever heard —

"You were incredible," he said. "I've never seen you in front of a room before. You were completely at home up there."

"That's who I am when I'm not afraid," she said. "That's who I want to be all the time."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"I know," he said, quietly. "I've been watching you get there."

She took his hand and held it tightly and didn't say anything else.
