# Chapter 4: The Trail of Trust

She'd said the trail was harder than it looked. She hadn't mentioned it was beautiful in a way that made you angry — that specific beauty of a difficult thing, where the effort and the reward arrive at the same time and you can't separate them.

They'd started at seven-thirty. Ethan had arrived at the trailhead to find her already there, leaning against her car with two coffees, wearing hiking boots that had clearly seen serious use. He'd looked at them and looked at his own, considerably newer, and she'd smiled in a way that said she'd noticed.

"You'll be fine," she said. "Probably."

"Reassuring."

"I'm not trying to reassure you."

She handed him the coffee and started walking before he'd finished his first sip.

The managing voice had offered, on the drive over, a full inventory of reasons this was inadvisable. She had catalogued them at the junction and kept driving anyway. Now she was climbing, and the trail demanded enough attention that the voice had gone quiet, which was its own kind of relief.

They'd been climbing for an hour. Maya set the pace — faster than Ethan expected and exactly what she should have expected of herself. She moved through the rocky sections with her head down and her hands out for balance, fluid in the way of someone whose body knew what her mind didn't need to manage. He followed, and neither of them talked much because the trail didn't invite it. It demanded attention.

At a flat section they stopped to drink water. He looked back at how far they'd come — the trailhead invisible now, the city a suggestion through the trees, the sky very blue and very close.

"How often do you do this?" he asked.

"When I need to think." She capped her bottle. "Or when I need to stop thinking. It works for both."

"What do you need today?"

She considered. "Neither, actually." She looked at him with something that wasn't quite a smile but was adjacent to one. "Today I just wanted to come up here."

She turned and kept walking. He watched her go for a second before following.

He was watching her when she stumbled.

A loose stone on a switchback, her right foot sliding — she caught herself before he reached her, but his hand was already on her arm. She straightened. They were close. The trail was narrow there, the drop to their left significant enough to matter, and he didn't let go of her arm immediately and she didn't step away.

"I'm fine," she said.

"I know."

He let go. They kept walking.

Twenty minutes later, without ceremony, she reached back and took his hand on a steep section. For balance, technically. Technically. Her grip was firm and she didn't look back at him and he didn't say anything. He just held on.

They stayed like that for the rest of the climb.

At one point she stopped to photograph a section of exposed rock face where the mineral layers ran in dramatic diagonals — pulled her phone out with her free hand, didn't let go of him with the other, shot it one-handed. He watched her compose the shot without thinking about it, her eye going to the interesting detail automatically.

"You've got good instincts," he said.

"You taught me about the lower left corner."

"Elara Voss taught you that."

She glanced back at him. "I suppose she did."

They kept climbing. The trail narrowed through a section of dense pine and then opened onto a long exposed ridge where the wind hit them properly for the first time and Maya tilted her face into it without breaking stride. Ethan watched her do it — that small unconscious pleasure, taken completely — and raised his camera.

She heard the shutter and turned.

"Did you just—"

"Sorry. Habit."

She looked at him for a moment. "Was that okay?"

"I should have asked."

"No — I mean—" She paused. "Was it a good shot?"

He lowered the camera and smiled. "Yes."

She turned back to the ridge and kept walking. He thought she was pleased, though she didn't say so.

---

The overlook opened up without warning — one more scramble over a rock shelf and then suddenly the city, spread out below them in the late-morning haze, the river a grey ribbon through the middle of it, the museum just visible in the cluster of downtown buildings.

Maya stood at the edge and looked.

Ethan stood beside her and looked at her.

She turned and caught him doing it. Didn't look away. "What?"

"Nothing." He sat down on the flat rock at the overlook's edge and opened his pack. "Hungry?"

They ate with their legs dangling over the drop, which Maya had insisted on and which Ethan had agreed to because he would have agreed to most things she suggested in that particular moment. The city hummed distantly below. A hawk turned lazy circles somewhere to the left.

She talked about places she wanted to go. Iceland first — the light in winter, the way photographers described it, flat and grey and unlike anything else. Then Japan, for the gardens and the specific quality of attention they demanded. She spoke about them the way she spoke about art, as though she'd already half-lived there in her imagination and was just waiting for the rest of her to catch up.

"Where would you go?" she asked.

"Anywhere with good light and someone worth photographing."

She looked at him. "That's not an answer."

"It is," he said. "It's just not the answer you expected."

She was quiet for a moment. Below them the city went about its business, indifferent and enormous. Up here it felt manageable. Up here it felt like the rest of the world had agreed to wait.

"I've never felt truly seen," she said. Not dramatically — just plainly, the way she said most true things. "I don't mean unseen in the way everyone feels sometimes. I mean — there are things about me that I've never shown anyone. That I don't think I can show anyone." She looked at her hands. "And I've gotten good at being the version of myself that works. That's easy. That doesn't require explaining."

He didn't fill it. He waited.

"Being around you makes that harder," she said. "The managed version. It keeps slipping."

"Good."

She looked up.

"Maya." He said it carefully, like he meant it. "I see you. The version that's here right now — the one who waited forty minutes at a window for the right light, the one who holds a pen she doesn't use when she's thinking, the one who brought me coffee without making it into anything." He paused. "I want to keep seeing you. Whatever that means. However long that takes."

She looked at him for a long moment. The hawk turned another circle. The city waited.

Then she leaned forward and kissed him.

It wasn't tentative. She'd decided, and Maya, when she decided, didn't hedge. Her hand came up to his jaw and she kissed him like she meant it, and he kissed her back with the same intention, one hand at the side of her face, the city and the hawk and the trail all dropping away to somewhere irrelevant.

When she pulled back she didn't go far. An inch, maybe. Her eyes still closed for a moment.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay," he said.

She laughed — that real laugh. She pressed her forehead briefly against his and then sat back and looked out at the city, and he sat beside her with his shoulder against hers and neither of them said anything about what was happening because there was nothing to say that the kiss hadn't already said more clearly.

The city sat below them, vast and indifferent and entirely beside the point.

He took his camera out of his pack. Film, black and white, loaded that morning.

"Can I?" he asked.

She turned to look at him. Her hair was loose from the climb, the city behind her, the light good the way it only got at altitude on clear mornings.

"Yes," she said.

He raised the camera. She didn't pose. She just looked at him — directly, steadily, the way she'd looked at the Diaz painting the night they met — and he pressed the shutter.

He lowered the camera.

She was already beside him again, shoulder warm against his, stealing the last of his lunch, pointing out a detail in the rock face below them that he'd missed. He let her take it. He was exactly where he wanted to be.

---

They started down an hour later, when the light began to shift and the air at altitude acquired its particular late-morning edge.

The descent was different from the climb — easier on the lungs, harder on the knees, and more conversational. Something about going down loosened the need to concentrate, freed up the part of the mind that talked. They moved through the pine section side by side where the trail allowed it, single file where it didn't, and the conversation arrived without effort.

She talked about her grandmother. A retired schoolteacher from County Kilkenny who had moved to the city when Maya's mother was small and never quite adjusted to it, who kept a garden on a south-facing balcony and grew things that had no business growing in the city and somehow managed it anyway. Who had died two years ago, quietly, in her sleep.

"She sounds like someone who knew exactly who she was," Ethan said.

"She was. It didn't look like certainty from the outside — it just looked like her. But yes."

She had also been a woman of simple, uncomplicated faith — the kind that didn't require defending because it had never been threatened. Maya had spent years since trying to understand how a person got to that. Whether it was something you were born into or something you arrived at. Whether it was still available to someone who had already lost it.

She didn't say any of this. But she thought it, stepping down through the last of the rocky section, her hand finding his again without ceremony.

"You have that," Ethan said. "The knowing-who-you-are thing."

Maya was quiet for a moment. The trail turned, opened briefly onto a long view of the valley.

"I have a version of it," she said. "A curated version."

He didn't push on it. He heard it and let it sit, the way he did.

They walked in silence for a while. The trail wound through a stand of birch, the light filtering through in the particular way of late morning, and she turned something over in her mind that she had been carrying for weeks.

"Can I ask you something personal?"

"Yes."

She kept her eyes on the path. "Have you ever been with a man? Or — thought about it?"

He was quiet for a moment — not caught off guard, she thought, just giving it proper weight.

"No," he said. "I've noticed men the way you notice people. But no." A beat. "Why?"

"I don't know how to explain it yet." She glanced at him sideways. "Maybe I will. Not today."

He nodded. Didn't press. The trail turned and she felt him let it go — filing it, she thought, not discarding it — and something in her chest loosened slightly, though she couldn't have said exactly why.

They came through the last of the rocky section and onto the wider lower path, the trailhead visible now through the trees.

"Same time next weekend?" she said.

"Different trail."

"Different trail," she agreed. "Harder."

"Obviously."

She glanced at him sideways. He was smiling. She didn't say anything else, and neither did he, and they walked the last ten minutes to the car in the quiet of two people who have run out of the need to perform for each other.
