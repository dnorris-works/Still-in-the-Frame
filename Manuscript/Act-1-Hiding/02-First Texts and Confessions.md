# Chapter 2: First Texts and Confessions

She told Zoe everything before the coffee finished brewing.

Zoe was already on the couch when Maya came out of her room — blanket pulled up to her chin, both hands around a mug, awake in the particular way of someone who had heard the apartment door the night before and filed it away to ask about in the morning. She didn't say anything. She just looked at Maya the way she always did when she was waiting — open, unhurried, making room.

Maya was not, as a rule, a person who talked about things. She processed privately, filed things away, returned to them alone at two in the morning when the apartment was quiet and there was nothing to distract her from her own thoughts. She'd been doing it since she was sixteen and had found that private suffering was at least controllable, which was more than she could say for the public kind.

But she told Zoe. She always told Zoe.

Maya stood at the kitchen counter in yesterday's socks, talking with her hands, and Zoe sat cross-legged on the couch with the expression she wore when she was trying to be calm about something she was not calm about.

"Okay," Zoe said, when Maya finally stopped. "Okay. So he's smart, he's interesting, he works in the same building, he noticed things about a painting that you've been studying for six months, and he asked for your number."

"Yes."

"And you gave it to him."

"Yes."

"Maya." Zoe set down her mug. "That's good. That's all good."

"I know it's good." Maya poured her coffee and didn't drink it. "That's the problem."

Zoe waited.

"When it's bad, it's easy. When it's bad I can just—" She made a gesture that meant she could see the ending from the beginning and simply not begin. "But last night was actually good and now I have to decide what to do with that."

"You text him," Zoe said. "That's what you do with that."

"What if he doesn't reply?"

"He gave you his number."

"What if he was just being polite?"

"Maya. He asked you for coffee."

She knew. She knew all of this. She sat down at the kitchen table with her phone face-down in front of her and knew everything Zoe was saying and it made no difference to the thing sitting in her chest that was not quite dread and not quite hope and was worse than either.

"I just don't want to want this too much," she said.

Zoe was quiet for a moment. Outside, the city was starting its Saturday — she could hear it through the window, the particular low frequency of a morning that had nowhere urgent to be.

"Can I say something?" Zoe asked.

"You're going to anyway."

"You've been doing this for two years. Meeting someone, finding a reason it can't work, walking away before they can." She said it quietly. "I'm not saying Ethan is different. I don't know that. You don't know that. But you can't know it if you don't find out."

Maya looked at her coffee.

"And for what it's worth," Zoe added, "the fact that you're this scared is interesting. You're not usually scared. You're usually just done."

That landed. Maya turned it over.

Zoe's expression shifted — something quieter moving through it. She picked up her mug.

"Text him," she said. "He's not Bret."

Maya looked at her phone.

The cursor blinked at her from a blank message field. She typed: *Hey, it was nice to meet you last night.* Deleted it. Too flat. She tried: *I keep thinking about what you said about the Diaz piece.* True, but it sounded like she'd been up all night thinking about him, which she had, but he didn't need to know that in the first text. She tried three more versions, each worse than the last.

Zoe appeared behind her, read over her shoulder, and said nothing.

Maya typed: *Last night flew by. Still thinking about what you said about lighting revealing hidden layers.*

She stared at it.

"Send it," Zoe said.

She sent it. Then she put the phone face-down again and drank her coffee too fast and burned her tongue.

Thirty seconds. A minute. She was reaching to flip the phone over when it buzzed.

She read his reply standing at the kitchen counter, and something in her chest that had been braced all morning quietly released.

"Well?" Zoe called from the couch.

Maya looked up. "He replied."

"Obviously he replied." Zoe was smiling. "What did he say?"

She read it aloud. It was three sentences about lighting and perception and a question about her thesis that was specific enough to prove he'd actually been listening the night before. Not a courtesy reply. Not a polite nothing. He'd thought about what she'd said and come back with something real. And at the end, almost as an aside: *My mother used to say that the right light doesn't just show you what's there — it shows you what was always there but hidden. I think that's what your thesis is about.*

She stopped reading.

"Okay," Zoe said.

"Okay," Maya said quietly.

She thought about his mother for a moment. Then she thought about her own — twenty-six years, and not once. She put the thought away before it could settle, the way she always did, and typed back.

---

Ethan was still in bed when his phone lit up.

He'd been awake for an hour, staring at the ceiling, thinking about something he was trying not to think too hard about. The self-portrait was still in the darkroom developing tray. He'd looked in on it twice this morning — technically, checking the print — except that both times he'd stood in the dim hallway and thought about something else entirely. Someone else, to be precise.

He was aware this was a significant amount of internal activity for a person he'd known for four hours.

He reached for the phone.

*Last night flew by. Still thinking about what you said about lighting revealing hidden layers.*

He read it twice. Then he got up and put the kettle on.

Jake arrived forty minutes later with two coffees and the energy of someone who had been waiting to have this conversation since Ethan's eleven o'clock text the night before. He was tall and loud in the way that made rooms feel smaller, and he sat down at the kitchen table without being asked because he had been doing that for three years.

"So," Jake said.

"So."

"You met someone. At the museum. After hours." He said it like he was narrating a film. "In the dark."

"The emergency lighting was on."

"Ethan."

"She works there. Takes visitors through the galleries. We talked about art for two hours and Dennis threw us out."

Jake picked up his coffee. "Walk me through it."

"I already texted you last night."

"You texted me three sentences. 'Met someone at the museum. Talked for two hours. Her name is Maya.' That's not walking me through it, that's a classified briefing." He settled back in the chair. "Start from the beginning."

Ethan walked him through it. The archway. The Diaz painting. The way she'd moved beside him without being asked when he'd suggested she change her angle. The reading room. Dennis. The steps outside. Jake listened the way he always listened — with his whole face, not performing attention but actually giving it.

When Ethan finished, Jake was quiet for a moment.

"She challenged you," he said. "When you said you knew what she meant about the museum being different at night. She called you on it."

"She asked if I actually meant it or was just saying it."

"And you liked that."

"I liked that," Ethan confirmed.

Jake nodded slowly. "Is she—"

"Yes."

"You haven't let me finish."

"You were going to ask if she's attractive."

"I was going to ask if she's interesting."

Ethan looked at him.

"Okay I was going to ask both." Jake grinned. "Well?"

"Both," Ethan said. "Significantly."

His phone buzzed on the table. He picked it up.

Maya had replied to his reply — something about the specific quality of museum light versus gallery light versus natural light, and whether a work changed depending on who controlled the conditions of its viewing. It was the kind of message that required actual thought to answer, which was not what he'd expected at nine-thirty on a Saturday morning.

"She's texting you right now, isn't she," Jake said.

"We're talking about art."

"You're smiling about art."

Ethan typed his reply. Maya came back within two minutes — a photograph this time, taken on her phone: a detail of a canvas he didn't recognize, the brushwork in the lower corner where most people never looked. The caption said: *Hidden narratives. They're everywhere once you start looking.*

He showed it to Jake without thinking about it.

Jake looked at the photo, then at Ethan, then back at the photo. "Okay," he said. "She's interesting."

"I know."

"Ask her to coffee."

Ethan typed while Jake was still talking.

Jake leaned over to read the screen. "Maya Vasquez. Two o'clock." He sat back. "Look at you."

"It's coffee."

"This afternoon."

A pause. Then: "Ethan. You have to go."

"I'm going."

He was already typing.

*I'd like to continue our conversation properly. Not with a security guard chasing us out. Coffee this afternoon? The Palette, two o'clock?*

Her reply came back in under a minute.

*I know the owner. We can stay as long as we want.*

He stared at that for a moment. Then typed: *See you at two.* Sent it before Jake could offer any further advice.

Maya sent back a single punctuation mark: a full stop. Which meant, he thought, that she was also trying not to say too much. He filed that away alongside everything else he'd been filing since last night — the tilt of her head, the precision of her attention, the real smile that arrived like weather changing, the way she'd asked her question directly rather than letting it sit.

"You've got it bad," Jake said cheerfully.

Ethan put his phone down. "I've met her once."

"Yeah." Jake stood and picked up his coffee. "Once was enough for you to text me about it at eleven at night. When do you ever text anyone at eleven at night?"

He didn't answer, because Jake was right and they both knew it, and there was nothing useful to add.
