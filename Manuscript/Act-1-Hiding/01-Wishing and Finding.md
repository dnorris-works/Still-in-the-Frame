# Chapter 1: Wishing and Finding

The photograph looked back at him and said nothing useful.

Ethan held the print up to the darkroom light — a self-portrait he'd taken three weeks ago on a Tuesday when the house was particularly quiet. Black and white, the way he always worked. The composition was correct. The framing was intentional. The light fell exactly where he'd positioned it to fall. And yet the man looking back at him from the paper looked like someone performing the idea of himself rather than being it. Technically accurate. Essentially false.

He'd been taking self-portraits for two years. They were all like this.

He set it in the tray and pulled the cord on the red lamp. The hallway light came on.

On the shelf above his desk, his mother smiled at him from a silver frame. He looked at the print drying in the tray, and then at her. The man in the print was technically himself. His mother, in the photograph, looked like someone who had never needed to perform anything.

He'd been trying to understand the difference for two years.

The Hartley retrospective — six weeks of installation work, opening in nine days — was two days behind schedule. He picked up his camera bag and his keys and locked the house behind him.

---

The City Museum of Contemporary Art was different after hours.

During the day it ran on murmured commentary and guided shuffling and the hushed reverence of visitors who weren't sure how close they were allowed to stand. At night, with the public gone and only the emergency lighting running its low amber through the galleries, the works felt different. More present. Less observed. Like people at a party who have finally stopped performing now that most of the guests have left.

Ethan had always liked that about them.

He set his equipment bag down in Gallery Seven and got to work. The Hartley retrospective opened in nine days and the lighting on the far wall was wrong — too warm, flattening the texture of the larger canvases instead of pulling it forward. He'd been raising it with the curatorial team for a week without resolution. Tonight he'd simply fix it and document the result.

He was adjusting the third track light from the small rolling ladder, his neck at an angle that would punish him tomorrow, when he heard movement in the next gallery.

He stopped.

The museum was supposed to be empty. Security swept at seven. The last gallery staff left at six-thirty. He checked his watch: eight forty-five.

He stayed on the ladder and listened. No alarm. No urgency. Just the quiet sound of someone moving slowly through a room — the unhurried pace of a person looking at something.

He finished the track light and the one after it, checked the effect from the center of the gallery, adjusted the angle two degrees, and checked it again. Twenty minutes, start to finish. When he climbed down for the last time and crossed to the archway between Gallery Seven and Six, she was still there.

She was standing with her back to him, about six feet from a mid-sized oil painting he'd installed himself three weeks ago — one of the newer acquisitions, a Diaz piece the director had been quietly proud of. She had a notebook open in her left hand and a pen she wasn't using in her right, and she was looking at the painting the way some people look at things they're trying to understand before they commit to having understood them.

He stood in the archway longer than was strictly necessary.

She was wearing dark jeans and a gray knit sweater, her hair pulled back loosely with a few strands at the side of her face. Her head was tilted slightly. Something about the angle reminded him of his mother in the photograph — that same patient, focused stillness.

He cleared his throat.

She spun around.

"Sorry." He lifted both hands. "I work here — Ethan Calloway, exhibition design. I didn't mean to—"

"I know who you are." She pressed her hand briefly to her chest, the notebook still open. "You installed the Ramos pieces last month. Third gallery."

He blinked. "You saw those?"

"I work here too. Part-time — I take visitors through the galleries, answer questions." She lowered her hand. "Maya Vasquez. I'm usually done before you start, apparently."

"I come in late when I need quiet." He nodded toward the wall behind her. "The lighting in the next gallery is wrong. I'm fixing it tonight." He paused. "What brings you in at this hour?"

She glanced at the painting. "Research. My thesis. Hidden narratives in abstract expressionism — artists who embedded personal histories into their technique rather than their subject matter." She paused. "That probably sounds exactly as niche as it is."

"No." He stepped into the gallery. "It doesn't."

She watched him approach the painting without stepping back.

"You know what I find interesting about this piece?" he said.

"Tell me."

"Most people read it left to right. The diagonal, the color tension in the upper left quadrant." He moved slightly to the right of where she'd been standing. "But Diaz was right-handed and worked with chronic pain in her shoulder, so she built from the center outward. If you stand here—"

Maya moved beside him without being asked.

"—the whole thing reorganizes. The center becomes the origin point instead of the midpoint. Everything else is aftermath."

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "Her notebooks confirm that. She called it *painting from the wound outward*. My argument is that the technique itself is the narrative — not a record of the wound, but the wound's actual shape made visible."

Ethan turned to look at her.

She met his eyes for a moment, then looked back at the painting.

"That's exactly what it is," he said.

She wrote something in her notebook. When she looked up he was still there, and neither of them moved toward the next thing.

She turned to a smaller Winters charcoal on the adjacent wall. He stayed. Her head went to that same tilted angle. The pen uncapped and then forgotten again.

"Do you always work this late?" he asked.

"When the museum's quiet." She capped the pen. "The daytime crowd changes the way I see things. Too much commentary in the air."

"I know exactly what you mean."

She looked at him directly. "Do you actually, or are you just saying that?"

He smiled. "I actually do."

---

They talked for two hours without meaning to.

It started with the Winters charcoal and moved through three other galleries and ended up, somehow, in the small reading room off the east corridor — sitting on opposite ends of the low bench that faced the wall of archival photographs. Grainy black-and-whites of men in suits standing in empty rooms, pointing at walls.

"I always thought this was the best room in the building," Ethan said.

"Because it's honest." Maya looked at the photographs. "It's not trying to be a gallery. It's just a room with pictures of people who cared about something."

He looked at her.

She seemed to hear what she'd said and looked at the photographs instead.

They discovered they'd both moved to the city for graduate school and stayed without fully deciding to. That they both preferred hiking to any gym ever designed. That they had completely incompatible opinions about a particular documentary series, which they argued about cheerfully for fifteen minutes and resolved nothing. That they both had a weakness for terrible wordplay, which they expressed with increasing competitiveness until she produced a pun about exhibition curation so precisely awful he had to put his head in his hands.

"That was genuinely terrible," he said.

"Thank you." She was smiling — a real one, unrehearsed, nothing like the measured politeness she'd offered him in Gallery Six. It changed her face entirely. He filed it away.

The security guard found them at ten forty-five. A large man named Dennis who had seen stranger things in eleven years on the night shift but delivered the message with appropriate gravity.

"Building closes at eleven. Just so you know."

They walked out through the main entrance. The city was cooler now, the Friday crowd thinned.

"I'm that way," Maya said, pointing left.

"Opposite direction."

She looked at her notebook, then at him. "Your lighting fix. Did it work?"

"I won't know until I see it in daylight tomorrow." He paused. "But I think so."

She nodded. Started to turn.

"Maya."

She turned back.

"I'd like to keep talking. Coffee, maybe — tomorrow, if you're not—"

"Yes," she said.

He smiled. "Okay."

She smiled back. The real one.

"Goodnight, Ethan."

He watched her walk to the corner and turn left, and stood for a moment on the museum steps with the city doing its Friday night thing all around him, thinking about light and composition and how some things only reveal their true structure when you change where you're standing.

He took out his phone and saved her number.

Then he walked home, and for the first time in longer than he could remember, the house did not feel particularly quiet.

---

Maya walked home the long way without deciding to.

The city was still warm, still moving. She passed the wine bar where a small crowd had spilled onto the pavement, the bookshop with its single lit window, the block where the Victorian terrace gave way to the newer buildings. She was thinking about the Diaz painting and what he'd said about standing at a different angle, and about the speed with which she had understood exactly what he meant — not only about the painting.

*Stop*, said the part of her that managed things. *You met a man in a museum. That's all this is.*

She kept walking.

She passed Riverside Community Church on Clement Street.

Through the tall windows the lights were still on — a meeting of some kind, figures moving inside, the warm amber of the interior, someone setting out chairs. She could hear faint voices, overlapping, the sound of a room where people felt at ease. She didn't slow her pace. She didn't stop.

But she looked.

She had been sixteen the last time she was inside a church that felt like that. Warm. Ordinary. Full of people who assumed without examining it that they belonged there.

She walked past and didn't look back. At the corner she turned right and took the short way home, and the managing voice settled back to its usual hum, and she told herself she was not going to think any more tonight about a man who stood in front of paintings and actually meant what he said.

She thought about him for most of the walk anyway.
