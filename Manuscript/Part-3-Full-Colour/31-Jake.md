# Chapter 31: Jake

Jake called on a Tuesday, which was unusual. He texted as a rule — had texted since they'd met at twenty-two, through a decade of shared exhibitions and bad choices and one good crisis each, and Ethan had come to understand that a call from Jake meant one of two things: something had gone wrong or something needed saying that Jake had decided he could not say well in a text.

Nothing had gone wrong.

"Thursday," Jake said. "The Crown. Seven. Just us."

It wasn't a question. Ethan said yes.

---

He had told Jake about the engagement by text, which he had known even as he sent it was the wrong medium. Jake had replied — *Brilliant. Really. Congratulations* — and then nothing else, which was not like him. Ethan had waited, had told himself it was fine, had told Maya it was fine, had sat in the darkroom on the Monday evening and let himself understand that it was not entirely fine and that he had been avoiding the understanding.

He had not told Jake before the ring. He had told Rachel and no one else, had kept it in the darkroom drawer while he waited for the right moment on the trail, and afterward he had texted Jake and Priya simultaneously and then called Rachel and then called Maya's phone just to hear her voice, and the order of those things was telling and he had known it was telling and had not wanted to examine what it said.

What it said was that he had not been sure how Jake would take it.

---

The Crown was the right pub for it. Low-ceilinged, good beer, a corner table they had been using since they were twenty-four. Jake was already there when Ethan arrived — again, Jake was always already there — with two pints and the particular quality of stillness that meant he was thinking about something specific.

Ethan sat down. Picked up the pint.

"Right," Jake said.

Not *how are you*. Not *congratulations again*. Just: right. The opening word of a conversation Jake had been having in his own head for long enough that he'd started without Ethan.

Ethan waited.

Jake turned his glass on the table. Once. Twice. He had a quality Ethan had always valued — he said the true thing and he said it plainly, without softening it into something easier to deliver or to receive. It had cost Ethan something, over the years, to be on the receiving end of it. It had also meant he always knew where he stood.

"Tell me about her," Jake said.

"You've met her."

"Tell me anyway."

Ethan looked at him. Jake was not looking back — he was looking at his glass, jaw set, the specific expression of someone who had decided to have the hard conversation and was now inside it without quite knowing how to move.

"What are you asking?" Ethan said.

"I'm asking you to tell me about her." He looked up. "I'm asking because I want to understand what I'm — what I don't understand yet. About what you're doing." He held Ethan's gaze directly. "I've known you for ten years and you have never, once, given me a reason to think you were anything other than exactly what you appear to be. Straightforward. Thoughtful. Occasionally infuriating. Good." A pause. "So I want you to tell me, because I want to understand."

The low sound of the pub around them. A burst of laughter from the bar.

Ethan set his glass down.

"She's intersex," he said. "She was born with a body that doesn't fit the categories. She has spent twenty-six years managing what people knew about her. She stopped doing that." He kept his voice even. "She put forty-two photographs of herself on a gallery wall and let the world look. She stood in front of a museum director she'd never met and said exactly what she was in clinical language because it was the right language for the room, and then she walked out into the street and laughed until she couldn't breathe." He paused. "She chose it, specifically, when she had every reason not to."

Jake was quiet.

"I know what she is," Jake said. "That's not—" He stopped. Picked up his glass. Didn't drink from it. "I'm not — Ethan. I'm not someone who would say she isn't worth it. I'm not someone who thinks there's something wrong with her." He set the glass down. "That's not what I'm trying to say."

"Then what are you trying to say."

The silence went on long enough that Ethan had time to catalogue the room — the table of women near the window, the barman changing a barrel, the particular smell of the Crown that had been the same since their mid-twenties and had become a kind of constancy he hadn't known he relied on.

"I went to the exhibition," Jake said.

Ethan looked at him.

"I didn't tell you. I went the second weekend, not the opening. I walked through the whole thing." He looked at his hands. "The photographs." He stopped again. When he looked up his expression was the one he wore when something had genuinely landed on him and he was still carrying the weight of the landing. "I didn't know how to look at them. At first. I stood in front of the third one — the one with you in it — and I thought: this is the most exposed thing I have ever seen a person do. And then I stood there for another five minutes and I thought: no. It isn't. The exposure is in the looking. The photographs just stand there. She's the one who chose to let them."

He drank his beer. Set it down with precision.

"I have not been—" He exhaled. "I have not been as good about this as I should have been. Since I found out. Since before the exhibition, actually. Since you told me."

Ethan said nothing. He was listening in the way he listened when something mattered — with his whole attention, not assembling a response while the other person was still speaking.

"I tried to talk to Priya about it," Jake said. "About how I was — how I felt. Uncomfortable." The word came out with some effort, as if he'd rehearsed it and it still wasn't quite the right word. "She told me I needed to sort myself out. In exactly those words. She was right." He turned his glass again. "I've been sorting myself out. Slowly. Not fast enough, obviously."

"Jake."

"I'm not finished." He looked up. "I want to say this clearly because I want you to be able to tell her. Not now — not as a thing I need credit for. But eventually. When it's right." He steadied himself. "What Bret Reynolds said to her. That's — I know about that, I know the shape of it from what you've told me, and that is the kind of thing I—" He stopped. Started again. "I've met Bret Reynolds twice. At the Alderton show, at something else. He's exactly what that makes him." He met Ethan's eyes. "What she did. Standing up in that room with a press lanyard three feet away, putting those photographs on the wall, choosing to be public before someone else could make it a story. I couldn't have done that. I don't think I know anyone who could have done that."

The pub went on around them. A chair scraped somewhere. The barman said something and the table near the window laughed.

"I called Rachel," Jake said. "Last week. She was..." He almost smiled. "She did not go easy on me."

"No," Ethan said. "She wouldn't."

"She told me I needed to decide what kind of friend I was going to be. Not to her — she was very clear that she wasn't asking on Maya's behalf. She was asking for mine." He looked at the table. "She told me the worst version of this wasn't that I said something wrong. It was that I said nothing. That I kept the distance and Maya noticed and had to carry the not-knowing." He looked up. "Has she? Noticed."

Ethan looked at him steadily.

"She knows," he said. "She's been carrying it without making it your problem, because that's what she does. She's very good at carrying things quietly."

Jake absorbed that. He looked like a man who had expected the honest answer and found it heavier than he'd prepared for.

"I sent a card," he said. "After the engagement."

"She kept it."

Jake looked at him. Something shifted in his expression — not relief exactly, more the specific expression of someone for whom a small thing has turned out to matter more than they'd budgeted for.

"She kept it," he said.

"She reads things twice when they land."

Jake was quiet for a moment. Then he said: "I want to meet her properly. Not the pub, not the opening-night crowd. Properly. The four of us — you, her, me, Priya. Somewhere she can see that Priya is — Priya. That we're not a thing she has to manage." He looked at Ethan directly. "And I want to apologise to her. Not a speech. Just clearly."

"She won't want a speech."

"I know. I'm terrible at speeches. You've seen me try." He picked up his glass at last. "I want her to know that I understand what she did, and that I'm — that I'm proud to know her. Which I recognise is not mine to feel. But I do."

Ethan looked at his oldest friend across the corner table of the pub they had been using for ten years. The good and the complicated of ten years, the specifics of this particular man — his directness, which was also occasionally his slowness, the loyalty that ran underneath it all like water under ground, the fact that he had come here tonight and said the hard thing instead of letting it become permanent silence.

"She'll like that," Ethan said. "She'll also probably make it easy for you, which you don't deserve."

Jake laughed — the short, surprised sound of a man caught off guard by his own relief. "No. I don't."

"She's good at that too. Making things easier than they need to be."

"I know." Jake looked at his pint. "I've seen how she is with you. I've seen it since the pub in November when you were both pretending you weren't already — whatever you were." He shook his head. "She makes you easier too. Have I told you that? You're—" He considered. "You used to keep things at a distance. Not people — you were never cold. But there was always the sense that the best part of you was somewhere else. Being saved." He looked at Ethan. "It isn't anymore."

Ethan thought about Maya in the kitchen that morning, her hands around a mug, telling him about the anthology Jordan had mentioned, the new project, the thing she was turning over. The particular quality of her voice when she was thinking aloud — not performing it for him, just letting him be in the room while she thought. He thought about how ordinary it was. How it had become ordinary without him noticing, and how that was the whole point of it.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

Jake raised his glass.

"To Maya, then," he said. Not ironically. Not with the distance of someone making a required toast. Straight, the way Jake said things he meant.

Ethan raised his.

"To Maya."

---

They stayed another hour. They talked about other things — Priya's case, a show at the Whitechapel that Jake had opinions about, the question of Ethan's next project which Ethan said was undecided and Jake said was clearly already decided. The conversation settled into the familiar register of two people who had known each other long enough to move between serious and easy without marking the transition.

Walking home, Ethan turned the evening over. Not with anxiety — with something quieter than that. The particular feeling of a thing that had been held at a careful distance for months finally brought into the room and looked at directly. Not clean. Not finished. But moved.

He thought about the card Maya had kept. The way she had read it twice.

She had been carrying Jake's distance without naming it to him — had absorbed it into the general category of things that might happen, things you knew were coming when you chose visibility, things you kept going in spite of. She had not asked Ethan to fix it. She had not made it his problem to solve.

He texted her on the corner of the high street: *Jake said he wants to meet you properly. The four of us. He's going to apologise. I told him you'd make it easier than he deserves.*

She replied before he'd reached the next block.

*He called it correctly in November, about you. I've always liked him for that.*

Then: *And for what it's worth — I've been waiting.*

He stood on the pavement in the dark and read it twice.

He put his phone in his pocket and walked home, and the autumn city was doing what it did — indifferent, continuous, its own life going on around him — and he let it, because all the things that mattered were not out here but in the warm kitchen where she would be when he got back, and they were fine, and the rest of it was beginning to be fine too.
