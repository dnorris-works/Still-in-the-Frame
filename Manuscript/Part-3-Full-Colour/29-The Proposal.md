# Chapter 29: The Proposal

It was his idea, but she said yes before he'd finished suggesting it.

He had texted on a Thursday evening — *the trail on Saturday?* — and she had replied before he'd put the phone down. She hadn't been back since the first time, nearly two years ago. She had thought about going back, in the abstract way you thought about places where significant things had happened to you, but the thinking had stayed abstract. Now it didn't.

She met him at the trailhead at eight. He had the same pack. The same worn boots. He looked up when he heard her on the gravel and the particular quality of his expression when he saw her — the one he didn't perform, the one that arrived before he could decide what to do with it — had never stopped making her feel the way it made her feel, which was seen, which was still the truest word for it.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning."

He handed her the second coffee, which he had brought in a flask because she had mentioned, once, almost a year ago, that the café at the trailhead was bad. She had not mentioned it since. He had remembered anyway.

That was the thing. He always had.

---

The trail was the same and entirely different in the way of places revisited — the path exactly as she remembered it, the rock section steeper than memory had made it, the pine section darker and more fragrant in the early morning than in the autumn. They climbed without hurrying. The city fell away below them in stages — the noise first, then the smell, then the sense of it, until there was only the path and the light through the trees and the sound of their breathing and the occasional bird doing whatever birds did in pines at eight in the morning.

She did not think about where they were going. She just climbed.

He was behind her through the narrow section and beside her when the trail widened, and she was aware of him the way she had been aware of him since the first night — the particular quality of his presence, the way he occupied space without demanding anything from it. He had not changed. Or he had changed, specifically, in the ways that mattered, in the ways that came from having chosen something and lived in the choosing of it. But the quality she had noticed in Gallery Six — the way he stood still in a room and actually looked — that had not changed. She thought it was the truest thing about him.

They stopped twice. Once at the rock shelf where she had sat and waited for the light on the first climb, and she stood at the edge and looked out over the valley below and he stood beside her and they didn't say anything because there was nothing to say that the view hadn't already said. Once at the water point halfway up, where a family was coming down — two children, a father in a yellow jacket — and they stepped aside to let them pass and the younger child, about four, stared at Ethan with the frank assessment of someone who had not yet learned not to, and Ethan looked back with equal gravity until the child grinned and looked away, and Maya filed it, as she filed most things about him, somewhere she could find it again.

---

The overlook was the same.

The flat rock, the long view, the city spread below in the particular haze of a clear Saturday morning. The hawk — or a hawk, she thought, the same hawk was statistically unlikely but she chose to believe it — turning a slow circle below the ridge line. The bench of stone on the far side where she had sat and said *I've never felt truly seen* to a man she had known for eleven weeks and understood that she was telling the truth.

She stood at the edge and looked out.

He came to stand beside her.

They were both breathing harder from the final section. She could feel the particular burn in her thighs that meant they'd climbed fast, and the clean cold air at altitude was doing what it did — making everything slightly more itself, slightly more present, the light sharper, the distances more accurate than they seemed from below.

"Same hawk," she said.

"Probably."

"Statistically unlikely."

"I know," he said. "Same hawk."

She looked at him. He was looking at the city.

She thought about the first time. The managed version of herself, still so fully assembled, still so careful — the version that said *there are things about me I've never shown anyone* and meant it as a warning and a test at the same time. She thought about what it had cost her to say that, and what it had cost him to answer the way he did, and what happened in the months between that afternoon and this one.

"I'm glad we came back," she said.

He turned to look at her then. And something in the way he looked — the particular quality of the attention, the way he was looking at her like she was the thing he had come to see, like the city and the hawk and the ridge were all beside the point — made her go still.

"Maya," he said.

He said it the way he had said it at the overlook the first time. Like he meant it. Like her name was a complete sentence.

He reached into his jacket pocket.

She understood before he moved, before she saw the ring, before he had done anything except say her name and put his hand in his pocket — she understood, with the particular certainty of someone who has been paying attention, what was happening.

He went to one knee on the flat rock.

The ring caught the light — a narrow gold band, a single dark green stone, the leaves at the shoulders of the bezel — and he held it up and looked at her, and his face was the face of someone who had thought about nothing else for three weeks and had still not found words adequate to the thing he was trying to say and had decided that was all right, that she would understand without them, that she always had.

His eyes were already wet. Not crying — holding it, the way he held things that mattered, back behind his eyes where she could see it but he hadn't lost it yet. She had seen that twice before. Both times something real was happening. This was the realest thing yet.

She put her hand over her mouth.

She had not planned to do that. Her body did it before she could decide — that reflex of someone trying to contain something too large for a normal expression. She stood on the flat rock where she had kissed him for the first time and looked at the man on one knee in front of her and felt everything arrive at once: the first night in the gallery, the trail, the darkroom, the pavement with its forty feet of city street between them, the exhibition and Bret's closed notebook, the church on a Sunday morning with her hand in his, all of it arriving in the space of one breath, and underneath all of it the one thing she had not let herself believe for most of her life — that she was someone a person could choose, completely, knowing everything, and not leave.

He was still looking at her. Waiting. Patient the way he had always been patient — not because he wasn't feeling anything but because he understood that she needed a moment, and he would give her as many moments as she needed, forever, that was the whole point of him.

"Maya," he said again. Quietly. Just her name.

"Yes," she said. Her voice came out rough. "Yes. Obviously yes."

Something crossed his face that she did not have a word for — relief and joy and something older than either, the expression of a man who has been hoping for something for long enough that its arrival feels almost impossible. He slid the ring onto her finger and stood and she went straight into him, her arms around his neck, her face against his jaw, and felt his arms come around her immediately, pulling her in, and he held her — really held her, the way he had on the pavement after three days of silence, tighter than comfortable, like he was making sure — and she understood that he had needed this as much as she had, had been waiting for it just as long, had been afraid of it in his own way, not of her answer but of the asking, of putting the whole thing into a gesture that could not be taken back.

She held on. He held tighter.

She was laughing and crying simultaneously, which she had not known was something she did until this exact moment. The sounds came out mixed together and undignified and entirely beyond her control and she found she didn't care at all, because his face when she pulled back to look at him was doing the same thing — the wet eyes, the jaw, the smile he couldn't contain — and they looked at each other on the flat rock at the top of the ridge and it was ridiculous and true and the best moment of her life.

"The ring," she managed.

"I know."

"The stone."

"I know." He was still trying not to fully smile and failing.

"It's your mother's brooch."

"Same stone. Not the same piece." He steadied. "I brought it home and then I went and found the brooch. I didn't plan it that way. I just — it was the same green." He looked at her hand. "I thought she'd have liked that."

She pressed her lips together against something that would have come out as more crying.

"She would have liked *you*," she said. "She would have liked this."

"Yes," he said. "She would have."

She looked up at him. His eyes hadn't dried. Hers hadn't either. The city was still there below them, the hawk was still doing its unhurried circles below the ridge, the morning was proceeding without them and entirely without concern for the significance of what had just happened on the flat rock.

"The church," she said.

"What about it."

"For the wedding. I want to get married at the church."

He looked at her. No hesitation, not even a flicker of it.

"Then that's where we'll get married," he said.

She put her hand against his jaw the way she had on the first day — the same gesture, two years on, the same decision underneath it — and kissed him the way she had kissed him then. Like she meant it. Like she'd already decided and there was nothing left to hedge.

When she pulled back the city was still there below them, vast and indifferent and entirely beside the point.

---

They started down in the late morning, when the light had shifted and the family in the yellow jacket had long since passed and the trail was theirs again.

She walked with her left hand out of her pocket. Not to look at the ring — or not only that — but because the air was warmer now and the descent was easier and there was no reason to keep her hands in her pockets anymore.

He noticed. He didn't say anything. He took her hand.

They went down through the pines, through the rocky section, back into the sound of the city below — the noise arriving first, then the smell, then the sense of it reassembling around them. She felt it come back and found, for the first time, that it didn't feel like returning to something constrained. It was just where they lived. Their actual life, waiting at the bottom of the hill.

She walked into it beside him, his hand around hers, the ring warm on her finger, the morning almost done.
