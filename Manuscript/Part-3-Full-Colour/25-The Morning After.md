# Chapter 25: The Morning After

She woke before he did.

The light was the particular early-morning quality of April — thin and clean, coming through the gap in the curtains at an angle that suggested the city outside was already awake but had decided not to make much of it yet. She lay still for a moment and listened to Ethan's breathing and the distant sound of a bus and let the night come back to her in pieces.

The room. The forty-two photographs. Bowen: *this is the thesis.* Zoe's arms, the particular warmth of that. Nancy's nod. Jordan and Diane near the entrance, staying until nearly the end. The woman she didn't know who had said *I didn't know this was possible.*

Bret's notebook, still empty, in his jacket pocket. The door swinging shut.

*It's done*, she had said.

She lay in the morning light and held that. She had expected to feel something more dramatic on the other side of it — some large release, the emotional equivalent of a door blown off its hinges. Instead there was this: the light through the curtains, Ethan asleep beside her, a quiet in her chest that was not the quiet of absence but the quiet of something settled. The particular feeling of someone who had survived something they expected to be worse and was still taking stock of the fact.

She got up quietly and went to the kitchen.

---

She was on her second coffee when he appeared in the doorway, barefoot, in the grey t-shirt he slept in, his hair doing what it always did in the morning. He looked at her with the slight unfocus of someone not yet fully assembled and then at the coffee on the bench and then back at her.

"How long have you been up?"

"An hour. Maybe more."

He crossed to the kettle without asking. She watched him move around his kitchen — her kitchen, she had started thinking, without deciding to — and said nothing, and neither did he, and the morning continued.

He made his coffee and came to sit across from her at the table.

"How do you feel?" he said.

She thought about it properly. "Like I put something down that I'd been carrying so long I'd stopped noticing the weight." A pause. "And a bit strange. Like the weight being gone is its own kind of thing to get used to."

He nodded. He drank his coffee. He didn't try to improve on what she'd said, which was one of the things.

They sat in the morning quiet and the light moved across the kitchen floor the way morning light moves — slowly, without urgency, as if the day had agreed not to press.

---

He got his camera at some point. She didn't notice when — she was reading the paper, or looking out the window at the courtyard below where a neighbour's cat was conducting a thorough investigation of the drain cover, and then she looked up and he had the camera and was looking at her through it.

She didn't pose. She looked back at him the way she had looked at the Diaz painting on the night they met — not performing attention, just actually attending. Trying to understand what she was seeing before she committed to having understood it.

He lowered the camera.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing." He set it on the table. "Just — you look different."

"Different how."

He considered. "You used to look like you were deciding something, even when you weren't doing anything. Like part of you was always mid-calculation." He picked up his coffee. "You're not doing that right now."

She sat with that. She thought it was probably true.

"Is it strange," she said, "to photograph someone you're with?"

"Yes and no." He turned the camera over in his hands. "It would be strange if I was trying to fix you somewhere. Hold a version of you still." He set it down. "But that's not what it is. It's more like — keeping track of someone who keeps becoming."

She looked at him across the table in the morning light.

"That's a good sentence," she said.

"Thank you." He was trying not to look pleased with himself and not entirely succeeding, which she found, as always, disproportionately endearing.

Later he opened his laptop on the kitchen table and showed her the photographs from the night before — not the exhibition prints, the new ones, the ones he'd made during the opening. She had not known he was making them. She had known he was there with the camera but she had been in the room the way she'd been in the room, present and not watching herself, and so the photographs were of someone she recognised but had not been observing.

She looked at them one by one.

There was one of her with Bowen — she was talking and her hands were slightly raised in the particular gesture she made when she was making a precise point, and her face was the face of someone who knew exactly what they were saying and was not afraid of it. There was one of her with Zoe, from just after the embrace, both of them still holding on a little, Zoe's eyes bright, Maya's chin on Zoe's shoulder and her eyes closed. There was one from later in the evening where she was alone near the east wall, her back partly to the camera, looking at one of the prints — looking at herself — and her posture was entirely open. Nothing braced. Nothing held back.

She looked at that one for a long time.

"This is what you see," she said.

"Yes."

She looked up at him. "When did you take this one?"

"About eight. You'd been talking to the woman who found you near the east wall and she'd gone back to the print and you'd stayed." He looked at the photograph on the screen. "You were just — looking. You didn't know I was there."

She looked at the photograph again. The woman in it was looking at herself without flinching. She was standing in a room full of people who knew who she was and she was not managing anything. She was simply there.

She closed the laptop gently.

"Keep them," she said. "All of them."

---

They walked after breakfast. No destination — just the Saturday city, the spring light, the trees coming into themselves along the park boundary. She had borrowed one of his jackets, which was slightly too large, and she kept her hands in the pockets and walked beside him and let the morning be what it was.

They passed the church.

She slowed without planning to. The wooden noticeboard had the Sunday schedule: morning service, ten o'clock. The warm light was not yet in the windows — it was too early, the building simply stone and glass in the April morning. But she looked at it the way she had looked at it in November, and the looking was entirely different.

"I want to go back," she said. "On Sunday. Not because I have to. Because I want to."

He had been watching the building too. He looked at her now.

"Tell me when," he said.

"Tomorrow."

He nodded once. "Then tomorrow."

They walked on. She walked beside him with her hands in his jacket pockets, the park to her left, the city to her right, the morning doing what it was doing. She didn't try to name it.

She didn't need to.
