# Maya — Character Profile

---

## The Essential Truth
Maya is not defined by her condition. She is defined by what she does with it: she became someone who understands hidden things. She reads art the way she reads people — looking for the true thing underneath the surface presentation. She found her vocation in the space between what is shown and what is real, because she has lived in that space her entire adult life. This is not tragedy. It is also her gift.

---

## Basic Information
- **Age:** 26
- **Occupation:** Art history graduate student; part-time gallery guide at the City Museum of Contemporary Art
- **Thesis:** "Hidden Narratives in Abstract Expressionism" — artists who concealed autobiographical truth in the formal language of abstraction; the cost of concealment; what revelation gives back
- **The thesis is about herself.** She knows this. She has always known this. She has not yet decided what to do with that knowledge.

---

## Physical Description
- Presents entirely as a woman — there is nothing visually unusual about her
- Intersex condition: **Complete Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome (CAIS)**
  - XY chromosomes; the body cannot respond to androgens; developed female in the womb as a result
  - External genitalia female; has a phallus (clitoromegaly) and a vagina
  - No uterus; no ovaries; internal undescended testes (surgically removed in adolescence)
  - Cannot become pregnant
  - Condition is X-linked recessive — inherited through Eileen, her mother, who is a carrier
- Physically fit; has hiked since her grandmother took her to the hills as a child; her boots are worn in ways new boots never are
- Dresses without performance — quality but not display; she is not trying to convince anyone of anything with her clothes
- Her face does something particular when she is genuinely interested in something: she goes very still and focuses completely; people who don't know her sometimes think she is cold; she is the opposite

## The Interior Life

### What she is like when she is safe
When Maya is with someone she trusts — Zoe, eventually Ethan — she is funny. Quietly, precisely funny. Her humour is observational and dry; she notices the absurd detail in a situation and names it without fanfare. This is not a performance. It is who she actually is. The reader should encounter this early and often — it is the truest version of her.

She is also, when safe, completely present. She listens the way she looks at paintings: full attention, no divided mind, no preparing her next sentence. People feel seen by her before she has said anything. She does not know she does this.

### What she is like when she is hiding
The managing voice. She has a running internal calculation — angle, light, clothing, positioning, the management of physical intimacy so that no one finds her before she decides to be found. This calculation runs beneath every interaction she has not yet decided to trust. It is exhausting. She has lived with it so long she no longer notices the exhaustion.

When she is hiding she talks about art. It is not deflection — she genuinely loves art — but it is also armour. She can spend two hours on Elara Voss and feel nothing has been at risk.

### The thing she does with her grandmother's voice
Her grandmother was the first person who ever made her feel that her body was not a problem to be solved. Her grandmother never knew the full truth — Maya didn't know the full truth until she was sixteen — but her grandmother's way of existing in the world (practical, warm, uncomplicated in her faith) left something in Maya that survived everything that came after. When Maya is frightened she sometimes hears her grandmother's phrase: *He sees all of you, love. Not the parts you show. All of it.* She does not know whether she believes this. She has not thrown the thought away.

## Medical and Family History

### The condition
Discovered formally at sixteen when she failed to menstruate. The doctors were clinical; the language was careful; none of it landed gently. Her mother was in the room. Her mother said nothing. Her father was not there — he had already stepped back from the harder conversations by then. The silence in that doctor's office set the pattern for the decade that followed.

### Eileen (mother)
Was informed when Maya was three weeks old. Has carried the knowledge as guilt ever since. Has expressed her love in every way except the one that mattered: naming the thing. Maya has felt the love and the silence simultaneously her whole life and has never been able to reconcile them. She does not yet know about Eileen's carrier status. When she learns it — in Part Three — it does not resolve the wound. But it changes its shape.

### Father
Decided when Maya was sixteen that this was a medical matter, now managed, and did not require further discussion. He was not cruel. He was simply not equal to it. He remarried when Maya was nineteen. His new wife does not know. He attends the wedding as a quiet witness to something he never fully understood.

### Bret
Two years before the story begins. She told him when she was too tired to keep running the calculation. His response was immediate and final: *"What the fuck. You're a freak. You're not a real woman. You deceived me."* He left that night. His parting words: *"Don't ever tell anyone. You'll ruin yourself."* She sat very still. She said: okay. She walked out. The shaking didn't start until the street.

She lost two things that night. She understood recently that it was two losses, not one: she lost Bret (who was not worth keeping), and she lost God (who might have been). She had prayed before she told him. That was the last time.

## Relationship with Faith
Her grandmother's faith was warm and uncomplicated — a God who made things and loved them. The church she encountered as a teenager was not that. The church she encountered as a teenager had clear categories for what a body was supposed to be, and she did not fit them, and it said so in a dozen small ways before it said so in any large one.

After Bret, she stopped entirely. Not in anger — in exhaustion. She did not have the energy to argue with a God who might agree with him.

The sticky note: at sixteen, before the Bret year, she copied a verse onto a sticky note and put it on her mirror. Psalm 139: *I praise you because I am fearfully and wonderfully made.* She took it down after Bret. She did not throw it away. She put it in a book. She has not looked at it since. She has not moved it either.

The faith arc of this novel is not about her returning to the church she left. It is about discovering that a different version of the thing was possible all along.

## Key Relationships

**Zoe**
Knows everything. Has known for three years. Zoe's response when Maya told her was to say nothing for a long moment, then: *"Okay. So. Are you hungry?"* Which was exactly right. Zoe's love is practical and unceremonious and bottomless. She has watched Maya hide and has not pushed — not because she doesn't care, but because she trusts Maya to find her own way out. Her crying first when Maya tells her about Ethan is not sentimentality. It is three years of relief.

**Ethan**
The first person whose attention Maya could not manage. She tried the usual methods — the art conversation, the curated version of herself — and none of them worked because he was not interested in the curated version. He kept looking at the thing underneath. She chose him in the moment she stopped the managing voice. She did not choose to tell him. Her body chose, and she stopped preventing it. The difference matters to her. She did not confess. She was found, and she stayed.

**Dr. Dana Bowen**
Sees Maya's thesis as genuinely important work. Has never asked a personal question. Their relationship is entirely professional and entirely sustaining — it is the one space where Maya is valued for her mind with no other dimension required. Bowen's push in the thesis meeting (*"Is the argument that concealment is valid, or that it's costly?"*) is the question Maya has been avoiding about her own life.

**Rachel**
Receives Maya with a warmth that is almost painful because it is entirely unearned — Rachel knows nothing and loves immediately. The Garrett story is Rachel's way of saying: *I see the thing in you that my brother sees.* Maya barely makes it to the end of the street before she has to stop and stand still on the pavement for a moment. This is the effect uncomplicated kindness has on someone who has been braced against it for years.

**Nancy Griffin**
Not intersex. Her authority is theological and pastoral — she has sat with enough people the institution rejected to know where it is wrong. She does not share a condition with Maya. She simply knows that God made her, and says so, and means it. The fact that she has no personal stake is what makes it land.

**Eileen**
See Eileen's profile. The relationship is not resolved in this novel. It is opened.

## Voice on the Page

Her dialogue when she is hiding: precise, a little formal, the art conversation as armour. She uses complete sentences. She does not trail off.

But the guardedness has a leak: she is constitutionally incapable of accepting a vague answer. When someone says something that could be genuine or could be performance, she asks directly. *"Do you actually, or are you just saying that?"* This is not aggression — it is the one place where the hiding drops without her deciding to drop it. She has been managing herself so carefully for so long that the need for truth slips out sideways. It is one of the first real things Ethan sees in her.

Her dialogue when she is safe: shorter. More gaps. She lets sentences end without filling the silence after them. She makes the dry observation and does not explain why it's funny. She trusts the other person to catch it.

Her internal voice: exact and unsentimental about herself. She does not catastrophise in her narration — she observes. *She had done this before: said yes to something before her body knew what her mouth was agreeing to.* The emotion arrives in the precision of the observation, not in the naming of the emotion.

She notices specific things in rooms: light sources, the quality of shadow, the arrangement of objects. This is the art historian's eye. It is also displacement activity — she is always partly elsewhere, partly in the painting.

---

## The Arc

**Part One:** She is hiding. She has perfected it. She is also, underneath the perfection, exhausted by it. Meeting Ethan does not immediately change this — it makes it worse before it makes it better, because he is the first person who has made hiding feel like loss rather than safety.

**Part Two:** She is found — privately, by Ethan, then publicly, by choice. Each step costs her something. She pays each cost. The faith wound reopens and begins, slowly, to close. The thesis resolves because she resolves.

**Part Three:** She is not fixed. She is integrated. The hiding is over. The managing voice is quiet. She can stand in a room and be looked at and not calculate anything. This is not triumph. It is ordinary. It is everything.

---

## What the Author Must Never Forget
Maya is not a subject. She is not a representative of intersex people. She is one specific woman with a particular way of seeing art, a precise dry humour, worn hiking boots, a grandmother's voice in her head, a sticky note she never threw away, and a capacity to love that survived everything the world did to make her believe she couldn't. Write her as that woman, completely, and the argument takes care of itself.
